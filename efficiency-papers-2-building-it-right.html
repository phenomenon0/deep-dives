BUILDING IT RIGHT

The inference-time wins are taken. The next order of magnitude is baked into the architecture before training begins._
The Efficiency Papers — Part II: Architecture (Series Finale)

01 // The Shazeer Bet

In November 2019, Noam Shazeer uploaded a seven-page paper to arXiv with no co-authors, no conference submission, and a title that winked at his own earlier work: "Fast Transformer Decoding: One Write-Head is All You Need." Shazeer was employee number two hundred at Google, co-author of the original Transformer paper, gold medalist at the International Mathematical Olympiad with a perfect score. He looked at the KV cache during autoregressive decoding and asked a question nobody else was asking: what if 31 of these 32 key-value heads simply did not need to exist?

The math told him why. During autoregressive decoding — generating text one token at a time — you cannot parallelize across sequence length. Each token depends on the last. The GPU sits there with thousands of cores, massively underutilized, waiting for the KV cache to load from memory. Shazeer computed the memory-to-arithmetic ratio: for standard multi-head attention during incremental inference, it approaches 1.0 when sequence length is comparable to model dimension or batch size is small. That means the GPU spends as much time loading data as doing actual multiplication. The arithmetic units are idle.

His answer was Multi-Query Attention. Keep all 32 query heads — let each head ask its own question. But share a single set of keys and a single set of values across all of them. In code, the change was removing one dimension from an einsum: P_k: [h, d, k] became P_k: [d, k]. The KV cache shrank by a factor of 32. Decoding got 12x faster. Perplexity increased by roughly 2% (10.2 to 10.4 for the base model). On WMT14 English-German translation, MQA actually achieved the highest BLEU score of 28.5 — better than the baseline. Training speed was essentially unchanged: 433ms versus 425ms per step. The KV projections are a tiny fraction of total compute. MQA cost nothing during training and saved everything during inference. The rare truly free lunch in machine learning.

The critical insight was about the bottleneck. On modern GPUs, compute capacity can be 100x higher than memory bandwidth in terms of operations per byte. During autoregressive decoding with standard MHA, the memory-to-compute ratio approaches 1.0, meaning compute is almost entirely wasted waiting for memory. MQA brings this ratio down by a factor of H (the number of heads), pushing the workload back into the compute-bound regime where GPUs actually shine. The GPUs were not slow. They were starving.

The paper sat ignored for four years.

Not because the idea was wrong. Because in 2019, nobody was serving billion-parameter models in production. Training was the bottleneck — getting bigger models to converge faster was the only problem that mattered. Encoder-decoder models still dominated: T5, BART. Shazeer's MQA numbers came from encoder-decoder setups where only the decoder half benefits, making the gains look modest. GPT-3 launched in June 2020 and showed that massive decoder-only models could do extraordinary things, but GPT-3 was an API — OpenAI ate the serving bill. The open-source community had not yet felt the pain of hosting these models themselves.

PaLM broke the seal in April 2022. Google's 540-billion-parameter model quietly used multi-query attention — Shazeer was a co-author. At 540B, the KV cache for standard MHA would have been enormous. PaLM proved MQA could work at massive scale without quality collapse. Then Falcon (2023, Technology Innovation Institute) adopted MQA with a pragmatic twist: 8 KV heads, one per GPU in an 8-way tensor parallel setup, avoiding the redundancy of broadcasting a single KV head across all devices. The Fireworks.ai team benchmarked Falcon and found up to 11x throughput improvement on longer sequences, 30-40% latency reduction under load, and 71x larger batch sizes for Falcon 7B. Those numbers were dramatically larger than Shazeer's original 1.8-2.4x because decoder-only models benefit fully, longer sequences amplify the savings, and the real-world gain is in batch size — you can serve far more users simultaneously.

The Middle Ground

On May 22, 2023, Joshua Ainslie and five other Google researchers published the GQA paper. The insight: Shazeer went too far and not far enough. Collapsing to a single KV head was unstable during fine-tuning — the paper's appendix revealed frequent loss spikes and divergence on long-input tasks. But the full 32-head setup was wasteful.

GQA split the difference. Instead of collapsing all H KV heads down to 1 (MQA), collapse them to G groups where 1 < G < H. Each group of H/G query heads shares one K head and one V head. GQA-1 equals MQA (one group, maximum compression). GQA-H equals MHA (H groups, no sharing). GQA-8 — the configuration they selected — sits in the middle.

Share K/V across groups of 8 query heads instead of all 32. Eight KV heads total. And the conversion recipe was stunning: take any existing MHA checkpoint, mean-pool the K/V projections within each group, retrain for 5% of the original compute. Six hundred TPUv3 chip-days to convert a T5-XXL model that took thousands to train from scratch. This meant the entire existing ecosystem of pre-trained models could be cheaply upgraded to GQA without starting over.

The numbers from the paper told the story cleanly. GQA-8 on T5-XXL ran at 0.28 seconds per sample versus 1.51 seconds for full MHA — 5.4x faster — while losing only 0.1 points of average quality across seven diverse benchmarks. MQA was marginally faster at 0.24 seconds but dropped 0.6 quality points and could not be trusted during fine-tuning. And here was the killer: GQA-8-XXL was faster than MHA-Large (0.28s vs 0.37s) while being substantially better at everything (47.1 vs 46.0 average). You get a bigger, better model that is also faster.

Why 8 groups specifically? The paper tested 1, 4, 8, 16, 32, and 64 groups. Going from 1 (MQA) to 8 adds only modest inference overhead. From 8 to 64, the cost curve steepens dramatically as you approach MHA. But the real reason is hardware: 8 KV heads means one head per GPU in a typical 8-way tensor parallel setup. Standard sharding replicates a single KV head across all partitions — pure waste. GQA-8 eliminates this by placing exactly one KV head on each GPU. The "optimal" number of groups is really the number of GPUs you have.

The adoption cascade was the fastest the field has seen. July 2023: Meta shipped Llama 2 with GQA in the 34B and 70B variants, 8 KV heads, 64 query heads. The smaller 7B and 13B kept MHA — GQA's benefit scales with model size. September: Mistral 7B used GQA with 8 KV heads even at 7B scale, combined with sliding window attention at a window size of 4,096. By 2024, every major open model — Llama 3 (GQA with 8 KV heads across all sizes from 8B to 405B), Gemma, Phi, Qwen — had adopted it. GQA became the default attention mechanism for the entire industry.

The irony

Shazeer left Google in October 2021 to found Character.AI, frustrated that leadership would not release his conversational chatbot Meena. PaLM used his MQA technique with him gone. GQA made his idea practical, written by other Google researchers. The adoption cascade happened while he was at a startup. In August 2024, Google paid $2.7 billion to bring him back. The MQA paper remains an arXiv preprint. Seven pages, no co-authors, no conference. Its core idea is now inside virtually every LLM on the planet.

The conversion recipe: how to retrofit GQA

The GQA paper showed you could convert any existing MHA checkpoint to GQA in five steps:

1. Take the H key projection matrices (one per head), each of shape [d_model, d_h].

2. Group them into G groups of H/G heads each.

3. Mean-pool the projections within each group: average the weight matrices together. This preserves the most information from the pre-trained model — the paper tested three methods and found mean pooling (55.5) beat selecting the first head (55.0) and random initialization (54.5).

4. Do the same for value projections.

5. Re-train for 5% of original training steps with the same dataset and hyperparameters. Roughly 600 TPUv3 chip-days for T5-XXL.

Critically, GQA achieves reasonable performance after conversion alone — with zero uptraining — while MQA requires uptraining to be useful. This is because GQA preserves more of the original structure. Both gain significantly from 5% uptraining with diminishing returns past 10%. The entire existing ecosystem of MHA models could be cheaply converted to GQA without retraining from scratch.

The Deeper Bet

Then DeepSeek refused the tradeoff entirely.

DeepSeek trained V3 on 2,048 NVIDIA H800 GPUs — chips designed to comply with U.S. export controls on high-end AI hardware. The H800 offers reduced NVLink interconnect bandwidth compared to the H100. Where Western labs could throw hardware at the problem (Llama 3.1 405B used roughly 10x more compute), DeepSeek had to think their way out. MLA was the crown jewel of this constraint-driven philosophy.

In May 2024, the DeepSeek-V2 paper introduced Multi-head Latent Attention. The question it asks is fundamentally different from GQA's. GQA asks: "how do we fit attention into less memory?" MLA asks: "what if compression itself makes the model better?"

Where GQA deletes heads to save memory, MLA compresses all of them. Every token's keys and values get projected through a low-rank bottleneck — a 5,120-dimensional input squeezed down to a 512-dimensional latent vector. That tiny vector is what gets cached. When the model needs the full keys and values, it reconstructs them on demand: each of 128 heads gets its own unique K and V, grown from the shared seed. It is like storing DNA instead of photocopying the organism. Every clone is unique, grown from the same compact code.

The ablations in Appendix D dropped a quiet bombshell. GQA performed worse than standard MHA. MLA performed better than MHA while using a KV cache equivalent to GQA with only 2.25 groups.

The compressed version outperforms the uncompressed original. This is not supposed to happen. The standard assumption is that compression equals quality loss. You are throwing away information. GQA confirms this — it is cheaper but slightly worse. MLA violates the assumption.

The hypothesis, supported by research on neural network singular values: low-rank compression acts as regularization. Neural networks develop a small number of very large singular values that carry generalizable knowledge, and a long tail of small ones that carry memorization noise. By forcing attention through a 512-dimensional bottleneck, MLA prevents the model from memorizing noise. It learns only what generalizes. As the planetbanatt analysis articulates: "we are essentially ignoring the nuisance space. We only want to learn things which are likely generalizable."

This reframes the entire story. MLA is not a cost-cutting measure that trades quality for efficiency. It is a quality improvement that happens to also save 93.3% of memory. The GQA approach — delete heads to save memory — is an explicit sacrifice. MLA compresses heads through a bottleneck and they emerge stronger. The analogy is lossy image compression that removes noise: the decompressed photo actually looks better than the original because the compression removed artifacts the eye never wanted.

And the compression enables something else. GQA forces groups of query heads to share identical K/V projections — like making eight students share two textbooks. They cannot each study different chapters simultaneously. MLA gives every head its own unique K and V, reconstructed from the shared seed. The low-rank decomposition allows the same latent information to be used in very different ways by different heads. Each head reads its own interpretation of the compressed state. Diversity is preserved. Redundancy is not.

There was a catch. Rotary Position Embeddings — the standard way modern LLMs encode token position — break when you compress the keys before applying them. RoPE works by rotating K and V vectors based on position before they are cached. But MLA compresses K and V into a latent before you would apply RoPE. The full K vectors are inaccessible at the time you would want to rotate them. You cannot rotate what does not exist yet.

DeepSeek's fix was elegant: Decoupled RoPE. Split each attention head into two components. A content vector flows through the compression bottleneck, carrying "what." A tiny 64-dimensional position vector bypasses compression entirely, carrying "where." The content part is reconstructed per-head — each of 128 heads gets its own unique key. The positional key is shared across all heads, MQA-style. Position is universal. Meaning is per-head.

MLA requires roughly four times more arithmetic than standard attention. But it is faster, because modern GPUs are memory-bandwidth-bound during decoding, not compute-bound. Moving less data beats doing less math. The H100 can execute 3,350 GB/s of bandwidth but nearly 2,000 TFLOPS of FP8 compute. Arithmetic is cheap. Memory transfers are the tax.

Making it tangible: at 128K context length with standard MHA, you need approximately 488 GB of KV cache. That is more than the memory of a single H100 (80 GB). You would need a minimum of seven GPUs just for the KV cache, with nothing left for the model weights or activations. MLA reads 576 floats per token instead of 16,384 — a 28x reduction. What was a multi-GPU memory crisis becomes something that fits comfortably alongside the model weights on a single node. This is what makes MoE viable: MLA frees the memory that 256 experts would otherwise need.

At inference, DeepSeek fuses the decompression matrix into the next matrix in the pipeline — no nonlinearity separates them, so you can precompute W_fused = W_next * W_UK once and apply it directly to the latent vector. The decompression step vanishes entirely during serving. The latent is used directly, as if it were always the right representation.

DSA: The Second Compression

MLA compresses what you store per token. But at 128K tokens, even compressed attention is O(L^2). DeepSeek's answer in V3.2 was a second compression: not what you store, but how many tokens you attend to.

A tiny "scout" model — multiple FP8 multi-head indexers running in a compressed space — pre-scores all past tokens. From 128K candidates, the indexer selects the top 2,048 by relevance. Only those 2,048 receive full-precision MLA attention. The other 125,952 are skipped entirely. O(L^2) dense attention becomes O(L * k) where k = 2,048 — a 62.5x reduction in attention computation.

The scout is trained in two phases. First, a dense warm-up: freeze the main model, train only the indexer using KL divergence loss to match dense attention patterns. 2.1 billion tokens, learning rate 1.0e-3. Second, sparse training: unfreeze everything, train end-to-end with the indexer selecting tokens. 943.7 billion tokens, learning rate 7.3e-6. Fifteen thousand steps of 480 sequences at 128K tokens each. The indexer learns to find the semantic needles in massive haystacks.

Quality? Statistical parity with dense attention. MMLU-Pro: 85.0 versus 85.0. GPQA Diamond: within noise. SWE-bench Multilingual: 57.9 versus 57.8. Fiction.LiveBench: V3.2 with DSA actually outperforms dense on some metrics. Per-token GPU cost cut by approximately 2x in both prefill and decode modes at 128K context. Reducing from 2,048 to 1,024 selected tokens shows only a 0.3-point degradation — the selection is robust. The model has learned which tokens matter and which do not, and its judgment matches brute-force dense attention almost perfectly.

The two compressions stack. MLA: 28x fewer values per token. DSA: 62x fewer tokens attended. Combined: a system that once needed hundreds of gigabytes of KV cache memory for 128K context can now operate comfortably on a single node. Attention goes from "impossible at 128K" to "practical and cheap."

This is the pattern that defines the entire article. Shazeer compressed the KV heads (2019). Ainslie found the middle ground (2023). DeepSeek compressed the latent representation (2024). Then DeepSeek compressed the number of tokens attended (2025). Each compression builds on the last. Each makes the next one possible.

And at no point — from MQA through GQA through MLA through DSA — did the models get worse. MQA had a 2% perplexity increase but massive speedup. GQA recovered almost all quality while keeping most of the speed. MLA beat the uncompressed original. DSA matched dense attention exactly. At every step, the compressed version matched or exceeded the uncompressed baseline. Compression is not a compromise. It is a design philosophy. And in the case of MLA, it is actively beneficial — the regularization effect of the low-rank bottleneck produces a model that generalizes better than one without compression.

The entire trajectory from MHA to MLA+DSA is a story about discovering that the KV cache contained enormous redundancy. Thirty-two independent K/V projections per head? Redundant — eight groups suffice (GQA). Eight groups? Redundant — a 512-dimensional latent suffices (MLA). All 128K past tokens? Redundant — the top 2,048 suffice (DSA). Each compression stripped away what was not needed, and each time, the model either did not notice or actively improved.

02 // The 26-Year Idea

In 1991, Robert Jacobs, Michael Jordan, Steven Nowlan, and Geoffrey Hinton published "Adaptive Mixtures of Local Experts" in Neural Computation. The idea was complete on page one: split a network into specialists. A gating network decides which specialist handles each input. They demonstrated it on a vowel discrimination task. Each expert learned a simple sub-problem. The divide-and-conquer logic was elegant. Soft competition through a learned gate. Conditional computation — not every parameter fires for every input.

Then the idea went dormant. For twenty-six years.

Not because the theory was wrong. Because the world was not ready.

Mixture of Experts requires spreading experts across devices and communicating between them. Every token must be dispatched to its chosen expert, processed, and the result gathered. This is an all-to-all communication pattern — the most bandwidth-intensive operation in distributed computing. In 1991, the fastest networks moved data at kilobytes per second. Modern InfiniBand moves data at 50 gigabytes per second. Even with that four-million-fold improvement, all-to-all communication accounts for more than 40% of total MoE training runtime on modern hardware. In the 1990s, the idea was utterly impractical.

There were also stability problems. Without careful load balancing, MoE collapses: the gating network develops a preference for one or two experts. Those experts get more training data. They improve faster. The gating network routes even more tokens to them. Other experts receive fewer tokens, learn slower, degrade. A self-reinforcing death spiral. The final state: two overloaded experts doing all the work, 254 dead experts contributing nothing. The model has effectively collapsed to a dense network with most of its parameters wasted.

The idea waited for the hardware.

And there was a fourth reason, perhaps the most important: the dense scaling era. From roughly 2012 to 2017, simply making models bigger and training longer kept working. The relationship between scale and performance was smooth and predictable. Double the parameters, see a reliable improvement. Double the training data, see another reliable improvement. Kaplan and colleagues would later formalize this as neural scaling laws. When brute force works and scales predictably, there is no economic pressure to be clever. Nobody invests in parameter-efficient architectures when parameter-inefficient architectures produce reliable returns.

MoE is fundamentally a solution to the problem "how do I get more knowledge without proportional compute." In the dense scaling era, nobody felt that problem acutely. Compute was the bottleneck, and labs threw money at it. The question "what if most parameters could sleep for most inputs?" is only interesting when you have more parameters than you can afford to activate.

The Resurrection

In 2017, Noam Shazeer — the same Shazeer who would publish MQA two years later — uploaded "Outrageously Large Neural Networks: The Sparsely-Gated Mixture-of-Experts Layer" to ICLR. Co-authored with Mirhoseini, Maziarz, Davis, Le, Hinton, and Dean, it demonstrated that you could have a 137-billion-parameter model that only activates a tiny fraction of its parameters per input. The architecture: sparsely-gated MoE layers interleaved between stacked LSTM layers, with up to thousands of feed-forward sub-networks. Noisy top-k gating added learned noise to the gating scores before selecting the top-k experts, providing both exploration and load balancing. More than a thousand times the model capacity with only minor computational overhead. The idea from 1991 finally had GPU clusters, TPU pods, and distributed training frameworks to match it.

The title itself was a statement of intent. "Outrageously large." The message was clear: parameter count no longer needs to equal compute cost. You can have a model that knows as much as a trillion-parameter dense network but thinks at the speed of a 13-billion-parameter one. The economics of intelligence had changed. And the paper's author list reads like a who's who of the field: Shazeer (MQA, original Transformer), Hinton (backpropagation, the 1991 MoE paper), Dean (MapReduce, TensorFlow, Google Brain). The people who built the infrastructure were the same people who finally had enough infrastructure to make MoE work.

GShard (Lepikhin, Lee et al., ICLR 2021) scaled MoE to industrial proportions: 600 billion parameters on 2,048 TPU v3 cores. The architecture used 2,048 experts across 36 layers, trained in just 22 core-years — four days on the full TPU cluster. A dense model of comparable quality (2.3B parameters, 96 layers) needed 235 core-years and still yielded lower quality. The sub-linearity was striking: scaling from 37.5B to 600B (16x the parameters) only increased cost from 6 to 22 core-years (3.6x the cost). The economics were undeniable. More parameters. Better quality. Disproportionately less compute.

But GShard used top-2 routing, meaning every token traveled to two experts. This doubled the communication cost and the expert batch size. When your training pipeline already spends 40% of its time on communication, doubling that overhead is painful.

Then William Fedus, Barret Zoph, and Shazeer published Switch Transformers in 2021. The insight was counterintuitive: route each token to just one expert instead of two. Everyone assumed top-2 was necessary for quality. It was not. Top-1 routing halved the router computation, halved the expert batch size, halved the communication cost. They scaled to 1.6 trillion parameters with a 7x pre-training speedup. They also achieved the first successful training of large sparse models in bfloat16 — previously, MoE required float32 for stability. The trick was selective precision: experts in bfloat16 for efficiency, the router in full precision for numerical stability. Small differences in gating scores determine which expert wins. Those differences cannot tolerate rounding.

In 2022, Yanqi Zhou and collaborators flipped the routing problem entirely with Expert Choice (NeurIPS 2022). The insight was a Copernican shift: instead of tokens choosing experts — which causes load imbalance because some experts are more popular — let experts choose tokens. Each expert examines the full batch and picks its top-k tokens to process.

The elegance is in what this guarantees. Load balance is automatic: every expert processes exactly the same number of tokens by construction. Important tokens — those that many experts find useful — can be processed by multiple experts simultaneously, getting richer representations. Trivial tokens might be processed by few experts or even none, saving compute for where it matters. No capacity factor needed. No tokens dropped. No auxiliary loss to fight the training objective. Expert Choice achieved a 2x training convergence speedup over Switch Transformer and GShard in 8B/64-expert models.

The trajectory from 1991 to 2022 is a study in infrastructure dependency. The idea never changed. What changed was the world's ability to run it.

The Communication Wall

There is a hard physical limit that every MoE system hits. When tokens are routed to experts on different devices, they must physically travel across the network. All-to-all communication accounts for more than 40% of total runtime in large-scale MoE training. For DeepSeek-V3 specifically, cross-node expert parallelism creates a roughly 1:1 computation-to-communication ratio. Half the training time is spent moving data, not computing.

This wall is why MoE was dormant for 26 years. It is also why MoE on commodity hardware is still difficult. TPU pods and NVLink-connected GPU clusters provide the high-bandwidth interconnects that make all-to-all tolerable. Without them, the tokens spend more time traveling than being processed. The experts are brilliant, but the mail system is slow.

The history of MoE is a history of four interlocking problems being solved, one at a time, across three decades:

1. Compute (solved ~2017)

GPU clusters and TPU pods provided the raw parallel compute. Shazeer showed >1000x capacity was achievable.

2. Communication (solved ~2020)

NVLink and InfiniBand provided the bandwidth. GShard proved 2048-device MoE was feasible.

3. Routing (solved ~2021)

Switch Transformer showed top-1 routing was sufficient. Expert Choice eliminated the need for capacity factors.

4. Balance (solved ~2024)

DeepSeek decoupled selection from weighting. Bias-based balancing achieved balance with zero training signal corruption.

The 26-Year Gap — MoE Papers vs. Infrastructure
The pattern

The best ideas in machine learning are often limited by infrastructure, not theory. MoE was fully conceived in 1991 and waited 26 years for TPU pods. MQA was published in 2019 and waited 4 years for inference to matter. The ideas are ahead of the hardware. The hardware catches up. Then everything moves at once.

The MoE story sets up everything that follows. DeepSeek's innovations in Chapter 3 are answers to MoE's two oldest problems: how do you route without collapse, and how do you communicate without drowning? But MoE is only one axis of the architecture question. The feed-forward network is where MoE lives — replacing the dense FFN with a sparse collection of experts. The attention mechanism is the other half of the Transformer block, and it has its own set of problems at scale. The other axis asks: what if attention itself is the wrong primitive?

03 // DeepSeek's Two Moves

By 2023, Mixture of Experts had proven it could scale. Mixtral 8x7B shipped in January 2024 as the first high-quality open MoE — 47 billion total parameters, 13 billion active, outperforming Llama 2 70B on most benchmarks with 6x faster inference. But Mixtral used the old playbook: eight large experts, top-2 routing, auxiliary loss for load balancing. Two fundamental problems remained unsolved. Coarse-grained experts waste capacity — each is a full FFN, billions of parameters, and a token activates the entire thing even when only a sliver of that knowledge is relevant. And auxiliary loss, the standard fix for expert collapse, fights the primary training objective. Too little and experts collapse. Too much and the model learns to balance load instead of learning language.

DeepSeek solved both. Not by incremental improvement, but by rethinking the assumptions.

Move 1: Fine-Grained Experts

The intuition is physical. Imagine hiring a full orchestra when you only need a violinist. Standard MoE does exactly this — each expert is a complete FFN with billions of parameters, and when a token is routed to it, the entire FFN fires even if only a fraction of that knowledge is relevant to the input. The solution is to hire smaller musicians who each specialize in exactly one thing.

The DeepSeekMoE paper (January 2024) made a simple but powerful observation: 256 small experts are better than 8 large ones. Each expert is 1/m the size of a standard FFN. The model activates 8 of 256 per token — each narrower, each processing a more specific type of knowledge. The core insight is that granularity enables specialization.

With 8 experts, each must be a generalist. A single expert might handle French syntax, medical terminology, and JavaScript promises — topics that have nothing in common except that the router assigned them to the same expert for lack of alternatives. The expert becomes a messy grab-bag of unrelated knowledge. With 256 experts, each can afford to be a true specialist: one for French syntax, another for Python list comprehensions, another for medical dosage calculations. The routing becomes meaningful. The specialization becomes real.

DeepSeek added one more trick: a single shared expert, always active for every token regardless of routing. This expert absorbs common knowledge — grammar, basic reasoning, frequent patterns that every token needs regardless of its specific content. Without the shared expert, common knowledge gets duplicated across many routed experts, wasting capacity. Each specialist carries a copy of "how English grammar works" alongside its actual specialty. The shared expert eliminates this redundancy by giving common knowledge a permanent home. The 256 routed experts are freed to hyper-specialize.

Think of it as one generalist doctor who handles 80% of cases — the sore throats, the routine check-ups, the prescriptions everyone needs — plus 256 specialist consultants activated on demand for the specific condition each patient presents. The generalist is always in the room. The specialists come and go.

Move 2: The Bias Trick

The Collapse Problem

The expert collapse problem is existential. It is the single biggest failure mode in MoE training, and every major system has confronted it. Without load balancing, the dynamics are predictable and destructive.

The gating network develops a slight preference for Expert A — perhaps because Expert A's initial random weights happen to produce slightly better outputs for common tokens. Expert A receives slightly more training data. It improves slightly faster than the others. The gating network notices the improvement and routes even more traffic to Expert A. Expert A improves further. Other experts receive fewer tokens, update their weights less frequently, fall behind. The gap widens. Within a few thousand training steps, Expert A handles 80% of all tokens while most other experts are effectively dead — contributing nothing, learning nothing, their parameters wasted. The model has collapsed to a dense network with most of its capacity unused.

A training run that collapses midway wastes hundreds of thousands of dollars and weeks of GPU time. The collapse can be sudden — the model may train normally for days before one expert begins dominating — and by the time it is detected, the run may be unrecoverable.

Every major MoE system — Switch Transformer, GLaM, Mixtral, the original DeepSeekMoE — uses some form of load balancing. This represents trillions of tokens of collective experience with the same problem. And every prior solution corrupted the training signal. Shazeer used noisy gating. GShard added an auxiliary loss. Switch Transformer tuned the loss coefficient carefully. ST-MoE added router z-loss. All of these add a competing gradient that pulls against the primary objective: the model is simultaneously trying to learn language and balance load. These goals conflict. A training run that collapses midway wastes hundreds of thousands of dollars and weeks of GPU time.

DeepSeek's auxiliary-loss-free balancing solved the problem by recognizing that selection and weighting are different operations that need not be coupled. Each expert i gets a bias term b_i, initialized to zero. When routing, the bias is added to the affinity score to determine which experts are selected — this is the selection decision, "who gets picked." But the actual gating weight — how much each expert's output contributes to the final result — uses the original, unbiased score. This is the weighting decision, "how much does each one count." The two decisions are decoupled.

After each training step, a simple rule updates the biases: if expert i handled more tokens than average, decrease b_i by a factor gamma (making it less likely to be selected). If expert i handled fewer tokens than average, increase b_i (making it more likely to be selected). The adjustment has no gradient. It does not flow through backpropagation. It does not interfere with the training signal in any way.

The bias changes which experts get picked. It does not change how much they contribute. The training loss stays pure. No competing gradients. No corruption of the expertise signal. Just a non-gradient nudge that prevents the death spiral.

There is a third question — one that challenges the Transformer's most fundamental assumption. What if attention itself is not the right primitive for processing sequences? This question was asked in the 1990s by control theorists, who had been describing dynamic systems with hidden state for decades. It was partially answered in 2020 with HiPPO, which showed how to give an SSM principled long-range memory. It was answered more fully in 2022 by S4, which showed SSMs could solve sequence tasks that defeated every prior architecture. And it was answered definitively in 2023 by Mamba, which made SSMs competitive with Transformers on language.

The answer created a new architecture family that is now merging back into the Transformer lineage. Not as a replacement, but as a complement. Not as a competitor, but as a mathematical dual. The story of state space models is the story of an idea from control theory crossing into machine learning and discovering that the two fields were studying the same problem from different angles.

04 // The Mechanical Clock

State space models come from control theory — the mathematics of dynamic systems with hidden internal state. Two equations describe any such system. The state equation describes how the hidden state h(t) evolves based on itself (matrix A) and the input (matrix B). The output equation describes how the state maps to what you observe (matrix C, plus optional direct feedthrough D).

The metaphor: navigating a maze. The "state space" is the map of all possible positions. The state equation says: given where you are and which direction you push the joystick, where do you end up next? The output equation says: given your position, what do you see? This is a continuous-time formulation — the state evolves smoothly, not in discrete steps. To handle discrete tokens (text, audio samples), you discretize using a learnable step size Delta and a zero-order hold: each token's value is "held" constant until the next token arrives, converting the discrete stream to a piecewise-constant continuous signal, processing it, then sampling back to discrete.

Dual Personality

The beautiful trick is dual personality. The same SSM can be computed two ways.

In recurrent mode, you process tokens one at a time. Each token arrives. The hidden state updates: h_t = A * h_{t-1} + B * x_t. The output is read: y_t = C * h_t. Then the next token arrives. This is fast at inference — O(1) per step, constant memory, no KV cache that grows with sequence length. But it is impossible to parallelize during training because each step depends on the previous one.

In convolutional mode, you unroll the entire recurrence into a single convolution kernel derived from A, B, and C. The kernel encodes the decay pattern: how much the first token influences the tenth, the twentieth, the hundredth. Because the kernel is fixed (A, B, C are constants), you can precompute it once and apply it to the entire sequence in parallel — O(L log L) complexity via FFT, fully parallelizable on GPU. Pre-Mamba SSMs used both modes: convolutional for training (fast, parallel), recurrent for inference (fast, constant memory). Best of both worlds, as long as your parameters do not need to change per-token.

In 2020, Albert Gu and collaborators published HiPPO (High-order Polynomial Projection Operators) at NeurIPS. The problem: with random A, the model quickly forgets earlier inputs. "Hello" disappears from the hidden state by the time you reach the end of a sentence. The same vanishing gradient problem that plagues vanilla RNNs. HiPPO constructs matrix A so the hidden state tracks the Legendre polynomial coefficients of the entire input history. Recent tokens captured precisely. Older tokens decaying gracefully. A mathematically principled way to compress an entire signal into a fixed-size state.

The key insight: HiPPO does not learn what to remember. It is a mathematical structure that optimally approximates the input history under a specific measure. The state becomes a compressed summary of everything seen so far, with the compression scheme derived from approximation theory, not from gradient descent. The difference between an SSM with random A and one with HiPPO A is the difference between a leaky bucket and an optimal compressor.

Two years later, Gu combined HiPPO with a Cauchy kernel trick and published S4 — Structured State Spaces for Sequences — at ICLR 2022, winning an Outstanding Paper Honorable Mention. S4 conditioned matrix A with a low-rank correction, enabling stable diagonalization and reducing the SSM computation to a well-studied Cauchy kernel. Dramatically faster than prior approaches.

The Long Range Arena results demolished everything. S4 averaged 80.48% across all LRA tasks while no baseline exceeded 60%. Most dramatically, it solved Path-X — a sequence-classification task at length 16,384 — where every prior architecture performed at near-random chance. Path-X requires tracking dependencies across the entire 16K sequence. Transformers could not do it. LSTMs could not do it. Attention mechanisms that claimed to handle long sequences could not do it. S4 cracked it.

Then researchers tried it on language. S4 fell flat. Apple's machine learning research group tested it directly: "State Spaces Aren't Enough: Machine Translation Needs Attention." The title said it all. S4 could track dependencies across 16,000 timesteps of synthetic data. It could not learn to translate a sentence.

The Problem

The failure was not about scale or training. It was structural.

In S4, matrices A, B, and C are fixed. They do not change based on what the input actually is. Every token gets processed through identical dynamics. S4 is a mechanical clock: it ticks at the same rate, with the same gears, regardless of whether the input is "the" or "quantum" or a period.

This creates two fatal failures.

Selective copying: given a sequence like [a, NOISE, b, NOISE, c, MARKER], the task is to copy out [a, b, c] — the signal tokens, ignoring the noise. An LTI model treats every token identically. It literally cannot distinguish signal from noise because its parameters do not respond to content. The convolution kernel is the same at every position. It processes "a" and "NOISE" with identical dynamics.

Induction heads: given the pattern "A B ... A" the model must predict "B." This requires content-aware gating — recognizing that the current "A" matches a previous "A" and retrieving what followed it. Fixed parameters cannot condition on content. They cannot implement "if this token matches something I saw before, recall the next token." The math forbids it.

S4 is a brilliant stenographer who transcribes at constant speed but cannot skim, skip, or focus. When the CEO says "this next part is critical," the stenographer does not change behavior. When there are ten minutes of throat-clearing, every word gets equal weight. When a piece of code has a critical bug on line 47 and boilerplate on lines 1-46, S4 gives every line the same processing intensity. Language requires selection — the ability to say "this token matters, that one does not." S4's fixed dynamics make this structurally impossible.

S4 worked brilliantly on audio, where every sample matters roughly equally and long-range patterns dominate. It worked on pathfinding tasks, where the signal-to-noise ratio is constant. It failed on language, where information density varies wildly from token to token. The word "quantum" carries more information than "the." A proper noun must be remembered exactly while a conjunction can be forgotten immediately. A model that treats them identically will never be competitive at language.

S4 solved the memory problem: how to remember over long distances. But it could not solve the selection problem: what to remember. Language requires both.

The structural ceiling

This is not a training problem or a scaling problem. It is structural. A linear time-invariant system mathematically cannot implement "if token is important: pay attention." The math forbids it. 05 // The Selective Revolution

In December 2023, Albert Gu and Tri Dao published "Mamba: Linear-Time Sequence Modeling with Selective State Spaces." The paper is one of the most influential architecture papers since the original Transformer — not because it replaced attention, but because it showed a viable alternative and forced the field to reconsider what was necessary versus what was merely conventional in sequence modeling.

The core innovation is six words long: make B, C, and Delta input-dependent.

The input token passes through a 1D convolution (which provides local context from adjacent tokens), then gets projected to produce B, C, and Delta dynamically — all three generated from the current token itself.

What each parameter controls when made selective:

B(x) — the input gate. Controls how much of the current token enters the hidden state. In S4, B is constant: every token writes to state with the same intensity. In Mamba, B is a function of the input: a proper noun gets a strong B (write firmly to memory), a filler word gets a weak one (barely register). The model learns what is worth storing.

C(x) — the output gate. Controls what the model reads from the hidden state. In S4, C is constant: every token reads the same summary of history. In Mamba, C is a function of the input: when generating the answer to a question, C can focus on the relevant part of the accumulated state, ignoring the rest. The model learns what is worth extracting.

Delta(x) — the step size, and functionally the attention gate of the SSM world. Controls the resolution of discretization. Large Delta means "focus on the current input, integrate it strongly into the state" — equivalent to high attention weight. Small Delta means "coast past this, let the state evolve on its own" — equivalent to near-zero attention. When Delta is large, old information is partially overwritten by the new token. When Delta is small, old information is preserved and the new token barely registers. This is the mechanism that gives Mamba judgment: the ability to decide, per-token, whether to attend or to ignore.

A stays fixed (diagonal). The stable base that ensures the state does not explode or collapse. A controls the state's internal dynamics: decay rates, how quickly older information fades, how the state's modes interact. Making A input-dependent would add complexity without proportional benefit — the selectivity comes from the gates around A, not from A itself.

Now the model has judgment. It can say: this token is a proper noun, write it firmly into state. This token is a filler word, ignore it. This token matches a pattern I saw earlier, retrieve what followed. The mechanical clock becomes a thinking system.

Notice the parallel to attention. In a Transformer, attention scores decide per-token importance — high attention weight means "this token matters for the current prediction." In Mamba, Delta does the same job. Large Delta means "write this strongly to state" — equivalent to high attention weight. Small Delta means "coast past this, it does not matter" — equivalent to near-zero attention. Same function. Completely different mechanism. The Transformer looks back at all past tokens and weights them. Mamba processes each token once and decides in the moment how much to remember. Attention is retrospective. Selection is prospective.

There is an important subtlety in what stays fixed. Matrix A — the state transition matrix that controls decay rates and internal dynamics — remains constant. Only B, C, and Delta become input-dependent. A controls how the state evolves between tokens: how quickly older information fades, how the state's internal modes interact. Making A input-dependent would add significant complexity without proportional benefit. The selectivity comes from the gates around A, not from A itself. A provides the stable base that prevents the state from exploding. B, C, and Delta provide the judgment.

The Painful Sacrifice

Making parameters input-dependent kills the convolutional representation. This is a real cost, not a technicality. The convolution mode works because you can precompute a single, fixed kernel from the constant A, B, C matrices. This kernel can be applied to the entire input sequence in one pass, using the Fast Fourier Transform for O(L log L) complexity — fully parallelizable during training, using the same highly optimized convolution operations that power all of deep learning.

If B and C change at every timestep, there is no fixed kernel. Each position would need its own kernel, which is just the sequential recurrence with extra steps. The mathematical elegance that made S4 fast is gone.

Mamba abandons convolution entirely and runs purely in recurrent mode. This was not a compromise — it was a necessary structural decision. You cannot have content-aware processing and a content-independent kernel. One or the other. Mamba chose awareness.

But recurrence is sequential. How do you parallelize training?

This is where Tri Dao's FlashAttention expertise becomes critical. The SSM recurrence h_t = A_t * h_{t-1} + B_t * x_t defines an associative binary operator. Associativity is the key: it means you can reorder the computations. Instead of processing tokens 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 sequentially (8 steps), you can compute (1,2) and (3,4) and (5,6) and (7,8) in parallel, then combine (1-2, 3-4) and (5-6, 7-8) in parallel, then combine (1-4, 5-8). Three steps instead of eight. O(log L) parallel depth instead of O(L). This is the parallel associative scan.

But the real innovation is not the math — it is the hardware awareness. The naive implementation would materialize the full expanded state (batch x dim x length x state_dim) in GPU HBM. That tensor is enormous. Instead, Mamba loads SSM parameters (Delta, B, C) directly from slow HBM to fast SRAM, performs discretization and the entire recurrence in SRAM, and writes only the final outputs back to HBM. The expanded intermediate state never touches main memory.

For backpropagation, the intermediate states needed for gradients are recomputed rather than stored — trading compute for memory, just like gradient checkpointing but at the kernel level. This is a deliberate choice: recomputation costs O(1) extra FLOPS per element, but saves O(N) memory transfers where N is the state dimension. On modern GPUs where memory transfers are 100x more expensive per byte than arithmetic per FLOP, recomputation is dramatically cheaper than storing and reloading. The fused selective scan is 40x faster than a naive implementation.

Mamba-2: The Duality

Six months later, Gu and Dao dropped a bigger revelation. The paper title was a provocation: "Transformers are SSMs: Generalized Models and Efficient Algorithms Through Structured State Space Duality."

The SSD framework proves a mathematical equivalence that unifies two fields. A state-space model with a scalar-times-identity state matrix — meaning all diagonal entries of A are identical — computes exactly the same function as masked self-attention with a 1-semiseparable causal mask. Not approximately. Not in the limit. Exactly. The same sequence transformation y = f(x) can be realized as an O(T) recurrence (the SSM view) or an O(T^2) matrix multiplication (the attention view). The connection goes through structured semiseparable matrices: the output matrix M of the SSM has a specific low-rank structure below the diagonal that exactly corresponds to a particular attention mask pattern.

What this means concretely: the same sequence transformation y = f(x) can be realized as a linear-time O(T) recurrence (the SSM view) or a quadratic-time O(T^2) matrix multiplication (the attention view). The connection goes through structured semiseparable matrices — the output matrix of the SSM has a specific low-rank structure below the diagonal that exactly corresponds to a particular attention mask pattern. When all the scalar A values equal 1 (no decay), the SSM collapses to standard causal linear attention: Y = (L * QK^T)V. SSMs and attention are endpoints on the same spectrum.

The practical consequence: by restricting A to scalar-times-identity (all diagonal entries identical), Mamba-2 can use matrix multiplication as the core primitive — which GPUs are spectacularly optimized for via Tensor Cores — instead of the custom scan kernels Mamba-1 needed. The chunked algorithm divides the sequence into blocks. Within each chunk, use the quadratic (attention-like) computation — fast, parallelizable, exploits Tensor Cores. Between chunks, pass state using the linear (SSM) recurrence — compact, sequential, carries context. This hybrid algorithm is faster than either pure approach.

Mamba-3: Complex Values

Mamba-3: The Fields Merge

At ICLR 2026, the latest evolution arrived. Mamba-3 introduced three innovations rooted in classical control theory.

First, exponential-trapezoidal discretization — a more expressive scheme than the zero-order hold or Euler methods used in Mamba-1 and Mamba-2. The recurrence captures richer temporal patterns per step.

Second, complex-valued state space. The state is modeled with complex numbers, where transitions act as rotations in the complex plane. This is mathematically equivalent to applying data-dependent Rotary Position Embeddings on the B and C projections. The SSM field and the Transformer field independently converged on the same mathematical object: rotary embeddings. Mamba-3 does not need custom kernels for complex arithmetic — it reuses the RoPE module already optimized in every Transformer library. The same math. The same code. Different derivation.

The payoff on the Parity task, a challenging state-tracking benchmark: Mamba-2 achieves 0.9% accuracy. Mamba-3 achieves 100%. Complex values crack state tracking.

Mamba-3 also introduced MIMO — Multi-Input Multi-Output dynamics that couple channels instead of processing them independently. Previous Mamba versions used SISO (single-input single-output): each channel processed independently, with an outer-product state update. MIMO switches to a matrix-multiplication state update, coupling multiple input and output channels. The result: the state is both more expressive (channels interact, sharing information) and more compact (half the state size for the same quality). This is a rare Pareto improvement — usually expressivity costs memory. MIMO improves downstream accuracy by an additional 1.2 percentage points at 1.5B scale, on top of Mamba-3 SISO's 0.6 point gain over the next-best model.

At 1.5 billion parameters, Mamba-3 beats the previous best non-attention model (Gated DeltaNet) by 1.8 points average. It achieves the fastest prefill and decode latency across all sequence lengths tested, versus Mamba-2, Gated DeltaNet, and optimized Transformers. The implementation is elegant: complex-valued dynamics reuse the RoPE module already optimized in every Transformer library. No custom complex-number kernels needed. You get complex arithmetic through rotation — the same mathematical trick the Transformer world already built.

06 // Hybrids Win

Pure Mamba solved selection and efficiency. But it has a structural ceiling that no amount of engineering removes: a fixed-size hidden state means lossy compression. A Transformer's KV cache is an explicit database — every past token stored, retrievable exactly. Ask "what was the 47th token?" and attention looks it up. An SSM's state is a 256-dimensional vector that compresses the entire history. The 47th token has been blended in with everything else. You cannot un-blend it.

This shows up in specific failure modes that researchers at Harvard's Kempner Institute documented in "Repeat After Me" (2024):

Exact copying over long distances — "repeat this paragraph verbatim 2,000 tokens later." A Transformer handles this trivially via attention: each word in the original paragraph is stored in the KV cache and can be retrieved exactly. An SSM has blended those words into its state along with everything else. By the time you ask it to recall, the original text has been compressed beyond recognition.

Phonebook lookup — given a name-number mapping seen once ("Alice: 555-1234, Bob: 555-5678"), retrieve the number for a specific name. This requires exact storage, not compression. The state cannot un-blend Alice's number from Bob's.

Complex in-context learning — tasks where the model must simultaneously track multiple independent facts and retrieve specific ones on demand. Few-shot examples. Instruction-following with many constraints. Anything where the model needs to "look up" rather than "understand."

The framing from the Goomba Lab is illuminating: SSMs are brains, Transformers are databases. A brain processes everything in real-time with finite memory — great for compression and understanding, terrible for exact recall. A database stores everything explicitly — great for recall, terrible for efficiency. The ideal system is a brain with access to a database.

The hybrid architecture gives you both. SSM layers process the bulk of the sequence efficiently, compressing context and handling the 90% of tokens that need understanding but not exact recall. Attention layers periodically checkpoint the important information into an explicit KV cache, enabling precise retrieval of the 10% of tokens that matter for exact recall.

The ratio between SSM and attention layers is the key design decision. Too much attention and you lose the efficiency gains that motivated the hybrid in the first place. Too little attention and recall degrades on tasks that require exact retrieval. The engineering challenge is finding the sweet spot — the minimal amount of attention needed to preserve recall while maximizing the efficiency of SSM processing. Different research groups converged independently on this question in 2024-2025, and their answers were remarkably consistent.

Jamba: The Pioneer

AI21 Labs built the first large-scale hybrid. Jamba (March 2024) interleaves Mamba and Transformer layers at a 7:1 ratio. Seven Mamba layers process the sequence cheaply, compressing context and handling the bulk of tokens that need understanding but not exact recall. Every eighth layer, a Transformer block acts as a checkpoint — examining the compressed state and adding precise retrieval capability through its KV cache. The model also incorporates MoE layers every two blocks for capacity without cost, bringing total parameters to 52 billion with only 12 billion active during inference. Context length: 256K tokens.

Think of it architecturally. The Mamba layers are the conveyor belt — they move the sequence forward efficiently, compressing and updating the hidden state in linear time. Every few layers, the conveyor belt passes through a quality control station: the attention layer, which opens up its KV cache database and performs precise lookups. The conveyor belt does not stop. The database query is fast because it only happens for 1 in 8 layers. The critical insight is that most tokens do not need exact recall most of the time. The 10-15% of processing that does need recall gets a Transformer layer. Everything else gets the efficiency of a linear-time SSM.

The trend line from 2024 to 2026 is clear, and it tracks the evolution of a field finding its equilibrium.

FP8 mixed-precision training halves memory and communication volume. The format is E4M3 (4 exponent bits, 3 mantissa bits), with tile-wise quantization at 1x128 or 128x128 element granularity. Online quantization computes scaling factors on the fly. Selective precision keeps the embedding layer, output head, gating modules, normalization, and attention operators in BF16/FP32 — the router stays in full precision because routing decisions are numerically sensitive. The experts themselves tolerate FP8 because their outputs are combined by the gating weights. Validated at 671B scale with less than 0.25% relative loss versus BF16 — the first proof that extreme-scale models can train in 8-bit precision.

Multi-Token Prediction (MTP) adds a separate 14B parameter module (bringing the total model to 685B) that predicts one additional token beyond the standard next-token prediction. The architecture: each MTP module consists of a shared embedding layer, a shared output head, an independent Transformer block, and a projection matrix, maintaining complete causal chains at each prediction depth.

This serves two distinct purposes. During training, predicting further ahead forces the model to build better internal representations. A model that only predicts the next token can cheat with shallow pattern matching — "the" is always a safe bet after "in." A model that must also predict the token after the next token needs deeper understanding of the sentence structure and meaning. The MTP loss weight starts at 0.3 for the first 10 trillion tokens (strong auxiliary signal during early training when the model is learning basic structure), then drops to 0.1 (lighter touch once the model has internalized the patterns).

During inference, the MTP module doubles as a speculative decoding draft model. Instead of generating one token at a time, the model proposes multiple candidate tokens in parallel, then verifies them all in a single forward pass. Tokens that the verifier accepts are kept; those it rejects trigger regeneration from that point. In practice, this can nearly double inference throughput because the verification step is cheap (it is just a normal forward pass on a slightly longer sequence) while the speculation step generates multiple tokens for the cost of one.

Seventeen papers. Thirty-five years from Jacobs (1991) to Mamba-3 (2026). Three lines of research — KV cache compression, sparse computation, and linear-time sequence modeling — all converging on the same lesson.

The researchers in this timeline did not set out to prove the same principle. Shazeer was solving a memory bandwidth problem. DeepSeek was solving a hardware constraint problem. Gu was solving a long-range dependency problem. Dao was solving a GPU utilization problem. They arrived at the same answer from different directions: the architecture must respect the machine it runs on. The machine rewards compression. It rewards sparsity. It rewards locality. It punishes waste.

The easy wins are taken. FlashAttention made attention fast. Quantization made weights small. PagedAttention made serving efficient. Those are inference-time patches applied after the model exists — optimizations you bolt on. They gave us one order of magnitude. Maybe two.

The next order of magnitude is different. It is baked in before the first gradient is computed. MLA compresses the KV cache before a single token is generated — the architecture decides what gets stored. MoE routes computation through 256 specialists instead of one monolithic FFN — the architecture decides what gets activated. Mamba replaces the quadratic attention mechanism with a linear selective scan — the architecture decides how information flows. These are not optimizations. They are the model. They determine what it will be capable of, what it will cost to serve, and how fast it can think.

Shazeer saw this first. A seven-page paper, no co-authors, 2019. Delete 31 of 32 KV heads. The field ignored it because inference did not matter yet. Four years later, every model on the planet used his idea.

DeepSeek saw it next. Compress instead of delete. The field doubted it because compression was supposed to lose quality. It gained quality instead. Then they compressed the experts: 256 small specialists instead of 8 generalists. Then they compressed the routing signal: bias instead of auxiliary loss. Then they compressed the precision: FP8 at 671 billion parameters. Then they compressed the communication: DualPipe overlapping everything. Five compressions, one system, $5.6 million.

Gu and Dao saw it deepest. The bottleneck is not attention. The bottleneck is not state spaces. The bottleneck is always memory movement. They proved it by making two different algorithms — attention and SSM — fast in the same way, for the same reason, and then proved the algorithms were mathematically the same thing. The difference between FlashAttention and Mamba's selective scan was never architectural. It was perspective.

The architecture is the optimization. And the best architectures are the ones designed around the machine they run on. This is not a temporary condition. It is a permanent constraint. Hardware will change — from GPUs to TPUs to custom ASICs to whatever follows. The memory hierarchy will persist. The gap between fast-and-small and slow-and-large memory will persist. The architectures that win will be the ones that respect that gap. NVIDIA will release the B200 and the B300. AMD will narrow the software gap. Google will iterate on TPU. Each generation will be faster. Each generation will maintain or widen the ratio between its memory levels, because physics does not change. Light takes time to travel. Electrons take time to charge. Memory that is close is fast. Memory that is far is slow.

That is the principle underneath everything. That is how you build it right.
