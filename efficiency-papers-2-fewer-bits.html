FEWER BITS, SAME BRAIN

A 72-billion-parameter model needs 144 GB of memory. Your GPU has 24 GB. The answer is hidden in what you can throw away._
The Efficiency Papers — Part II

01 // The Weight of Intelligence

A model’s entire knowledge is stored as numbers. GPT-2 has 124 million of them — 250 megabytes in half-precision floating point. You can download it in the time it takes to make coffee. Qwen 2.5 72B has 72 billion numbers — 144 gigabytes. That’s more than most hard drives. DeepSeek-V3 has 671 billion — 1.3 terabytes. To run it, you need a server room.

These numbers are called weights. Each one is a floating-point number — a decimal with a sign, an exponent, and a fraction. In the FP16 format, that’s 16 bits per weight: 1 bit for sign, 5 for exponent, 10 for the fraction (mantissa). This gives you roughly 65,000 distinct representable values. Enough precision that the 15th and 16th digits of pi would survive the encoding.

The formula for how much memory a model needs is embarrassingly simple: parameters × bytes per parameter. Two bytes per weight at FP16. Multiply and weep.

DeepSeek-V3671B~1.34 TB~335 GB

But here’s the question that drives this entire article: how much information does a weight actually carry?

A Single Weight at Every Precision

Imagine measuring the width of a door. You get out a micrometer and record 914.4000 millimeters — seven significant figures. But you’re just checking if your couch fits through. You needed two figures. Those extra five decimal places are wasted precision. You paid for a micrometer when a tape measure would have told you everything you needed to know.

Neural network weights are the same. They’re stored to 65,000 levels of precision. But after training, most of them cluster in a narrow band around zero. The vast majority are quiet background noise — the numerical equivalent of silent musicians in a vast orchestra. A few outliers carry real signal. The dominant instruments, the ones the audience actually hears.

What if you could represent each weight with just 16 values instead of 65,000? That’s INT4 quantization. Four bits per weight instead of sixteen. One quarter the memory. And it works far better than it has any right to. A 72B model quantized to INT4 retains roughly 95–98% of its FP16 quality. The symphony still sounds recognizable. The bridge still stands. The measurement is close enough.

The central surprise of quantization: the information content of a trained neural network weight is far less than the bits we store it in. Most of those bits are noise. The art is figuring out which bits are signal.

The Scale Problem, Made Physical

GPT-2 at 250 MB fits on a phone, a Raspberry Pi, a browser tab. No quantization needed. This is the “fits anywhere” tier.

Qwen 2.5 7B at 15 GB needs a gaming GPU. An RTX 3090 or 4090 has 24 GB. Quantize to 4-bit and it drops to about 4–5 GB with overhead — suddenly a $300 GPU runs a real chatbot.

Qwen 2.5 72B at 144 GB is impossible on consumer hardware. You’d need 2x A100s (80 GB each) or 6x RTX 4090s (24 GB each). That’s $30,000+ in hardware. Quantize to 4-bit and it drops to 36–48 GB with overhead — a single A100, or two RTX 4090s. A $2,000 home setup runs what used to need a server room.

DeepSeek-V3 at 1.34 TB needs a data center. Eight H100s (80 GB each = 640 GB) aren’t enough at FP16. It’s an MoE model — 671B total parameters but only 37B activated per token — so the compute is manageable. But all 671B parameters still need to be in memory. At 4-bit, it fits on 8x A100s.

Why Shrinking Works

The drop from 65,536 representable values to 16 sounds catastrophic. It isn’t. And understanding why is the key to everything that follows.

FP32 is a ruler marked in tenths of a millimeter. FP16 is marked in millimeters. INT8 is marked in centimeters. INT4 is marked in inches. You lose fine-grained precision at each step, but the rough measurement — “about three inches” — is usually close enough. Neural networks, it turns out, are building bridges, not measuring gravitational constants. They need a few significant figures, not many.

After training, neural network weights follow a bell curve centered near zero. The vast majority live in a narrow band between -0.1 and +0.1. Only rare outliers — a fraction of a percent — have large magnitudes. Think of a 65,536-color palette being used to paint a stark line drawing with only a few distinct regions. Most of those colors are wasted. The weight distribution looks more like a line drawing than a sunset.

This means most weights don’t need many bits. If 95% of your values fit in a tiny range, you can represent that range with a handful of evenly-spaced levels and barely lose anything. The rare outliers — the few weights with large magnitudes — carry disproportionate information. They’re the model’s “strong opinions.” Modern quantization techniques specifically protect these outliers while aggressively compressing everything else.

Think of JPEG compression. JPEG throws away high-frequency visual detail your eyes don’t notice. Quantization throws away numerical precision the network’s downstream layers don’t notice. Both are lossy. Both exploit redundancy. JPEG exploits the fact that human vision is more sensitive to brightness than color. Quantization exploits the fact that neural networks care about the direction of weight vectors, not their exact magnitude.

The evidence goes deeper. Neural networks are massively over-parameterized. This isn’t a bug — it’s how training works. You need billions of parameters to navigate the loss landscape during training, but the final solution uses far fewer degrees of freedom. The Lottery Ticket Hypothesis (Frankle & Carlin, 2018) proved you can prune 90% of a network’s weights with minimal accuracy loss. Dense networks contain small subnetworks — “winning tickets” — that, if trained in isolation from the right initialization, match the full network’s accuracy. The implication: most parameters are scaffolding, not structure.

Later, Microsoft would train a model where every weight is literally -1, 0, or +1 — 1.58 bits per weight — and it matches models using 10x the storage. The actual information content per weight in a well-trained LLM might be less than 2 bits. We have been storing 14 bits of noise per weight this entire time.

Why are neural networks so redundant? One perspective: the loss landscape is smooth enough that many different weight configurations lead to effectively the same function. The network has many “equivalent” solutions, and the exact values are arbitrary. Quantization, in this view, isn’t damaging the network. It’s snapping each weight to a nearby equivalent value. Another perspective: the knowledge lives in weight ratios and directions, not absolute magnitudes. If a layer has weights [0.342, -0.118, 0.891], the network cares about the relative pattern. Quantizing to [0.33, -0.11, 0.89] preserves it. The direction of the vector is nearly identical. The magnitude shifted slightly. The downstream computation barely notices.

This is why quantization at 4 bits preserves 95–98% of model quality. You’re not destroying information. You’re rounding to the nearest useful value, and the nearest useful value is almost always close enough. The exceptions — the outliers, the sensitive weights, the dimensions the model uses for steering — are what the rest of this article is about. Handling those exceptions is what separates naive rounding (which breaks models) from modern quantization (which barely touches them).

02 // The Outlier Discovery

In August 2022, Tim Dettmers at the University of Washington was doing something nobody thought was exciting. He was trying to make INT8 quantization work for large language models. Quantization research, as he’d later put it, was “like printers — nobody cares about printers, nobody likes printers, but everybody is happy if printers do their job.”

Dettmers had an unusual background. Before his PhD, he’d spent three years in factory automation — routing data in food and milk processing plants. He studied psychology alongside his technical work. At FAIR, he ran 1.3-billion-parameter baselines on 128 GPUs each and still felt compute-constrained. He’s been described as independent-minded and sincere — someone who thinks creatively and challenges conventional wisdom.

He set out to make quantization “just work” at scale. A plumbing project. The kind of work that doesn’t win awards or generate Twitter buzz. But what he found along the way was far more interesting than the plumbing. He stumbled onto something about how large transformers organize themselves internally — emergent features that nobody had predicted or observed. The plumber discovered a secret room in the basement.

The Phase Transition

Small models quantized fine. At 125 million parameters, INT8 was trivial. At 1 billion, still clean. At 2.7 billion, some minor degradation appeared — nothing alarming. Dettmers kept scaling. Then at 6.7 billion parameters, something broke.

Below 6.7B, some transformer layers had outlier activations — values far larger than normal — but they were disorganized. Only about 60% of layers agreed on which hidden dimensions held the outliers. The dimensions shifted between batches. Everything was probabilistic and messy.

Then at exactly 6.7B, the model snapped into order. Suddenly, 100% of layers coordinated through the same outlier dimensions. Every layer “knew beforehand” which dimensions would hold the extreme values. Six feature dimensions — out of thousands — carried activations 100x larger than everything else. Seventy-five percent of all sequence dimensions were now affected by these extreme-magnitude features.

The numbers are vivid. Normal activation values in these models look like: [-0.10, -0.23, 0.08, -0.38, -0.28]. Nicely distributed. Manageable. The outlier dimensions look like: [-60, -45, -51, -35, -67]. Monsters. And the magnitude kept growing with scale: roughly 15 at 6B, 60 at 13B, 95 at 66B. The bigger the model, the louder the outliers screamed.

This wasn’t gradual. It was a phase transition, like water freezing. One moment, disorder. The next, crystalline coordination. Below the threshold, outliers were scattered and random — different layers, different dimensions, shifting between mini-batches. Above it, every layer locked onto the same six dimensions. The model had spontaneously organized itself.

The Steering Wheel

What were these outlier dimensions doing? Dettmers found they acted as the model’s steering wheel.

In a 6.7B transformer with sequence length 2048, there were roughly 150,000 outlier values per sequence. But they concentrated in only 6 feature dimensions out of thousands. That’s approximately 0.1% of all input features. A quantization error analysis made the damage clear: a vector WITH outliers showed a mean error of 0.12 after INT8 rounding. A vector WITHOUT outliers showed 0.015. The outliers caused 8x more quantization error than everything else combined.

Dettmers tried clipping them. Setting those 6 dimensions to zero. The result was devastating:

With outliers

Top-1 softmax probability: ~40%

Perplexity: normal

Model: coherent

Outliers zeroed

Top-1 softmax probability: ~20%

Perplexity: 600–1000% worse

Model: shattered

The model didn’t get worse. It broke. The outliers were consumed by the softmax nonlinearity in the attention mechanism, controlling which tokens attended to which. They functioned as a feature masking system — the model used these giant activations to suppress irrelevant information across layers. Without them, the softmax distribution became nearly uniform. The model couldn’t focus. Like ripping the steering wheel out of a car that’s still moving at highway speed.

And these features weren’t designed. Nobody told the model to use 6 dimensions as steering channels. They emerged spontaneously from the training process, only after the model crossed a critical size threshold. Below 6.7B, the model managed attention through scattered, ad-hoc mechanisms. Above it, the model invented a privileged coordinate system. This is an emergent property of scale, and Dettmers was the first to see it clearly.

The Mixed-Precision Fix

The solution, which Dettmers called LLM.int8(), was elegant precisely because the outliers were so systematic:

# LLM.int8() — mixed-precision decomposition
for each hidden dimension:
    if magnitude >= 6.0:
        route to FP16 matrix multiply    # 0.1% of values
    else:
        quantize to INT8                 # 99.9% of values
combine both outputs
# Result: zero quality loss. Half the memory.

The threshold of 6.0 was empirically chosen — it cleanly separates the two populations. At that threshold, only about 0.1% of values route to FP16. The computational overhead is negligible. The memory savings are real: half the memory of FP16 for the 99.9% of values that go to INT8.

It ran OPT-175B and BLOOM-176B with zero performance degradation on a single server with consumer GPUs. This was the first time anyone had run inference on models of this scale with INT8 quantization and no quality loss at all. Andrej Karpathy called it “Beautiful work (as usual)” and highlighted the “emergent features” questions it opened up.

The paper, “LLM.int8(): 8-bit Matrix Multiplication for Transformers at Scale,” was published at NeurIPS 2022. Its lasting significance isn’t just the quantization method — it’s the discovery of emergent outlier features, which every subsequent quantization paper had to reckon with. The outliers are there. You must handle them. How you handle them defines the method.

The discovery

Dettmers didn’t just solve a quantization problem. He revealed that large transformers self-organize. Past a critical size, the model spontaneously develops a privileged coordinate system — a small set of dimensions that become highways for steering attention. Nobody designed this. It emerges from scale alone.

03 // The Weight Surgeons

Dettmers had opened the door. He’d shown that naive quantization fails because of outliers, and that a simple mixed-precision decomposition can fix it. But LLM.int8() only got you to 8 bits. The memory savings were 2x. For the models people actually wanted to run on consumer hardware, they needed 4 bits. That’s 4x memory reduction. And 4-bit quantization is a harder problem — you’re representing each weight with just 16 possible values instead of 256.

Within months, three more research groups walked through the door Dettmers had opened, each wielding a different scalpel. All four methods share one insight: not all numbers are equally important. But they found the important ones through radically different lenses.

GPTQ: The Hessian Surgeon

October 2022. Two months after Dettmers’ paper, Elias Frantar at IST Austria asked a clean mathematical question: if I round this weight, how much damage does it cause? Not all rounding is equal. Some weights, if perturbed, cause the layer’s output to shift dramatically. Others can be rounded freely without consequence.

The answer lives in the Hessian matrix — the matrix of second partial derivatives of the reconstruction error with respect to the weights. Weights with high Hessian values are fragile. Weights with low Hessian values are sturdy. The Hessian tells you where the model’s sensitivity landscape has cliffs versus where it has gentle slopes.

GPTQ processed weight matrices column by column. For each column: quantize using round-to-nearest, measure the error, then redistribute that error to all not-yet-quantized columns, weighted by the inverse Hessian. Later columns absorb the mistakes of earlier ones. It’s like a surgeon who cuts in one place and immediately adjusts surrounding tissue to compensate, column by column, left to right, error flowing forward and getting absorbed.

GPTQ descended from a rich lineage. Optimal Brain Surgeon (1993) showed you could use second-order information to decide which weights to prune. Optimal Brain Quantization (OBQ) applied the same idea to rounding: quantize one weight at a time, in order of quantization cost, and compensate after each step. Mathematically elegant. Practically absurd — processing 175 billion weights one by one would take several hundred hours on modern hardware.

GPTQ’s breakthrough was a change in granularity: process columns instead of individual weights. A column of weights (say, 4096 values in a hidden dimension) can be quantized in one pass, and the error can be redistributed across remaining columns in a single matrix operation. This enables massive parallelism. Add mild dampening (1% of the average Hessian diagonal) to prevent numerical explosions from accumulated updates, and use Cholesky decomposition for the inverse Hessian to keep everything stable.

The speed headline: OPT-175B and BLOOM-176B quantized to 3–4 bits in four GPU hours. For comparison, the linear extrapolation of OBQ’s per-weight approach would need several hundred hours. GPTQ was 100x faster. The math isn’t just elegant — it’s the difference between “possible in a weekend” and “impossible in a month.”

It was published at ICLR 2023 and became the first practical sub-8-bit post-training quantization method for LLMs. The 175B model fit in a single GPU for generative inference, running at 3.25x speedup on an A100 and 4.5x on an A6000. For LLaMA-2 7B at INT4 with group size 128, GPTQ achieved 5.63 perplexity versus the 5.47 FP16 baseline — a gap most users cannot perceive in conversation.

SmoothQuant: The Channel Balancer

November 2022. Guangxuan Xiao at MIT’s Han Lab was staring at two distributions side by side and noticed something the previous researchers had overlooked.

Activations were spiky — a few channels had values 100x larger than the rest. Trying to quantize them to INT8 was like trying to fit a skyscraper and a cottage into the same size box. The range was too wide, and the fine-grained values got crushed into a few coarse levels.

Weights were smooth — nicely bell-shaped, easy to quantize. They fit into INT8 without drama. The quantization difficulty was completely lopsided: one side easy, one side impossible.

His insight: what if you could transfer the difficulty from one side to the other?

Imagine a seesaw. On the left: the activation distribution, with tall spikes jutting up. On the right: the weight distribution, flat and manageable. The seesaw is tilted hard to the left. SmoothQuant introduces a per-channel scaling factor that pushes the seesaw toward balance. Divide the spiky activation channels by a factor s. Multiply the corresponding weight channels by the same s. The spikes shrink on the activation side. The weights get slightly spikier — but they had headroom. Now both sides fit into INT8.

Published as SmoothQuant, the mathematical trick is a single line: Y = (X / s) @ (s * W). The output Y is identical to the original Y = X @ W. You’re just rearranging which side of the multiplication holds the difficult numbers. It’s a bookkeeping trick — invisible to the model, transformative for the hardware.

# SmoothQuant — the seesaw trick
# Y = X @ W  (original)
# Y = (X / s) @ (s * W)  (identical output, smoother both sides)
s_j = max(|X_j|)^0.5 / max(|W_j|)^0.5   # per-channel scale
X_smooth = X * diag(s)^-1                 # activations become calm
W_smooth = diag(s) * W                     # weights absorb the spikes
# Now BOTH fit in INT8 → use INT8 tensor cores → real speedup

The alpha parameter controls the seesaw balance. At alpha = 0.5, difficulty is split evenly between activations and weights. This works for most models — OPT, BLOOM, and the LLaMA family all do well here. At alpha = 0.75, more difficulty migrates to the weight side, which helps models with particularly severe activation outliers like GLM-130B. The intuition: if the activation spikes are truly extreme, you need to absorb more of them into the weights, even though that makes weights harder to quantize. There’s a sweet spot, and it’s typically found with a quick grid search over alpha values from 0 to 1.

At alpha = 0, you’re not smoothing at all — activations stay spiky, weights stay smooth, and you’re back to the original problem. At alpha = 1, all difficulty transfers to weights, which makes weights as spiky as activations originally were. Neither extreme works. The magic lives in the middle.

Why does W8A8 matter so much? Weight-only quantization (W4A16, W8A16) still needs FP16 compute for activations. The GPU loads smaller weights, but the actual matrix multiply still runs in FP16. SmoothQuant enables W8A8 — both weights AND activations in INT8 — which unlocks INT8 tensor cores on NVIDIA GPUs, Intel CPUs, and Qualcomm DSPs. That’s the difference between “smaller model” and “actually faster inference.” Results: up to 1.56x speedup, 2x memory reduction, negligible accuracy loss. A 530B model that previously needed 16 GPUs could serve from 8. SmoothQuant was presented at ICML 2023.

AWQ: The Activation Scout

June 2023. Ji Lin, also at MIT’s Han Lab, asked a question that sounds almost naive: how should you decide which weights are important? The obvious answer: look at the weights. Big weights are important. Small weights are disposable. This is what most people assume. It is almost completely wrong.

Lin ran an experiment on OPT-6.7B at INT3 with group size 128 that produced the most devastating table in quantization research:

MethodPerplexityVerdict
FP16 baseline10.86Perfect
Round-to-nearest (naive)23.54Broken
Keep 1% FP16 by weight magnitude22.37Barely helps
Keep 1% FP16 by activation magnitude11.36Nearly perfect

Read that again. Protecting the biggest weights does almost nothing. Protecting the weights that the largest activations flow through recovers nearly all quality. The weights that look important are not the ones that are important. The activations tell you what matters.

The fix was elegantly simple. Instead of keeping salient weights in FP16 (which requires mixed-precision hardware support), AWQ (Activation-Aware Weight Quantization) scaled up salient weight channels before quantizing. If a weight is 0.3 and you scale it to 3.0 before rounding to the nearest 4-bit level, the rounding error becomes proportionally 10x smaller relative to the weight’s magnitude. The activations get divided by the same scale factor, so the output is identical. It’s the same algebraic trick as SmoothQuant, applied with surgical precision to just the weights the activations care about.

AWQ won the MLSys 2024 Best Paper Award. It needed a 10x smaller calibration set than GPTQ. It was robust across domains — when you switched calibration data from one domain to another, AWQ’s perplexity varied by 0.5–0.6, versus GPTQ’s 2.3–4.9. And it was adopted everywhere: FastChat, vLLM, HuggingFace TGI, LMDeploy, and nearly every major serving framework.

In community benchmarks on LLaMA-2 13B at 4-bit, the methods are nearly indistinguishable: AWQ 4.33 perplexity, Q4_K_M (GGUF) 4.33, GPTQ 4.34. The differences are in the noise. The real differentiators are calibration robustness (AWQ wins), inference speed (depends on your kernel), and ease of use (GGUF wins for local, AWQ/GPTQ for serving).

The Shared Insight

All four methods converge on one truth: not all numbers are equally important for model quality. But they identify the important ones through different lenses, and the evolution of “what counts as important” tells a story about the field’s growing sophistication.

The evolution of “importance”

LLM.int8() (Aug 2022) said: some activations are huge — don’t touch them. Brute force, but it works. GPTQ (Oct 2022) said: some weights are fragile — if you must round them, compensate elsewhere. Surgical redistribution. SmoothQuant (Nov 2022) said: the problem isn’t the numbers, it’s which side they’re on — move the difficulty. Algebraic rebalancing. AWQ (Jun 2023) said: forget the weights entirely — the activations tell you what matters. Activation-aware philosophy. Each method is more elegant than the last. All started from the same observation: naive quantization destroys a tiny fraction of values the model relies on disproportionately.

04 // The GGML Revolution

September 2022. A medical physicist named Georgi Gerganov, working from an apartment in Sofia, Bulgaria, started writing a tensor library in C. He called it ggml. It had no dependencies, no Python, no GPU requirement. He described himself as “a non-AI-believer” when it came to neural networks. He was building it, he said, “mostly for fun.”

His personal website is a pure project portfolio — no bio, no social proof, no LinkedIn-style career narrative. Just a wall of side projects: acoustic keystroke sniffers, terminal UIs, Morse code decoders, a Bulgarian Wordle clone. The guy just builds things. His stated philosophy: “I prefer things to be super-minimal and without any third-party dependencies.”

Gerganov follows in the tradition of Fabrice Bellard, the legendary French programmer behind FFmpeg, QEMU, and TCC. One person, C code, absurd ambition. ggml was directly inspired by Bellard’s LibNC tensor library. The lineage runs deep: from Bellard’s philosophy of minimal, dependency-free C to Gerganov’s implementation of tensor algebra with strict memory management, multithreading, zero runtime allocations, and zero dependencies.

In October 2022, OpenAI released Whisper, their speech recognition model. Gerganov recognized the transformer architecture resembled GPT-2, which he’d already partially implemented in ggml. He realized: “Okay, I can easily port this. It might be interesting to have it running on a CPU.” He ported it. whisper.cpp ran faster than the Python original on an M1 Mac. The open-source community took notice. But nobody anticipated what came next.

Then came the earthquake.

Seven Days

On February 24, 2023, Meta published the LLaMA paper and began distributing weights to approved researchers through a gated access process. You needed to apply, get approved, and sign an agreement. On March 3, somebody uploaded the weights to 4chan. Within hours, they were on BitTorrent. Within days, every AI community on the internet had them. The weights that were supposed to be gated were now everywhere.

Seven days later, on March 10, Georgi Gerganov committed the first version of llama.cpp.

One person. One week. A 7-billion-parameter language model, quantized to 4 bits, generating text at human reading speed on a MacBook CPU. No GPU needed. No Python. No CUDA. Pure C/C++, with 4-bit quantization implemented from scratch using the ggml tensor library he’d built as a side project months earlier.

His README was characteristically modest:

“The main goal is to run the model using 4-bit quantization on a MacBook. [...] This was hacked in an evening — I have no idea if it works correctly.”

The next morning, developer Simon Willison ran it on his laptop and wrote: “As my laptop started to spit out text at me I genuinely had a feeling that the world was about to change, again.” He called it LLMs’ “Stable Diffusion moment.”

Twelve days after the first commit, the Changelog podcast noted llama.cpp was growing in GitHub stars faster than Stable Diffusion — which had itself been a rocket ship. The Hacker News community would later summarize: “He pretty much kicked off the revolution in March 2023.”

Think about what happened in those seven days. A single developer, working from Sofia, ported an entire large language model architecture to run on consumer hardware with 4-bit quantization, in pure C/C++, with no GPU dependency. Meta had teams of dozens, purpose-built infrastructure, and a multi-million-dollar compute budget. Gerganov had a MacBook and the ggml tensor library he’d written as a hobby. The asymmetry is staggering.

But it makes sense when you consider what ggml already was. The tensor library handled the low-level algebra — matrix multiplies, element-wise operations, memory management. The Whisper port had already proven the architecture could handle transformer inference in C. LLaMA’s architecture (self-attention + feedforward + RMSNorm) was structurally similar to what Gerganov had already built. The 4-bit quantization was a straightforward extension of block quantization. Each piece existed. Gerganov assembled them in a week because he’d been building the components for months.

Block Quantization: How It Actually Works

The core operation is deceptively simple. Take a group of 32 weights. Find the maximum absolute value — that’s your scale. Divide every weight by the scale, round to the nearest 4-bit integer, store the results plus the scale factor. To reconstruct: multiply each integer by the scale.

# Q4_0: the simplest block quantization
block_size = 32
for block in chunks(weights, 32):
    scale = max(abs(block))                # 1 FP16 number
    quantized = round(block / scale * 7)  # 32 x 4-bit integers
    store(scale, quantized)
# 32 weights = 16 bytes (data) + 2 bytes (scale) = 18 bytes
# That's 4.5 bits per weight on average

Simple. But crude. Why per-block and not per-tensor? Because weight distributions vary dramatically within a single tensor. One block might have values ranging from -0.01 to +0.01. Another might span -0.5 to +0.5. A global scale wastes precision — the small-value block gets mapped to a tiny subset of the 16 available INT4 levels. Per-block scaling lets each group of 32 weights use the full range. The smaller the block, the better the fit, but the more overhead from scale storage.

And different regions of a model need different precision entirely. The attention value projections — where the model encodes what information to extract from each token — are far more sensitive to rounding than the feedforward gate projections. The embedding and output layers, which map between token IDs and the continuous vector space, are fragile. Treating them all identically wastes bits where they’re not needed and starves them where they are.

K-Quants: The Hierarchical Leap

The K-quant system introduced a two-level hierarchy. Instead of one scale per 32 weights, it uses superblocks of 256 weights containing 8 sub-blocks of 32 each:

K-Quant Superblock Structure 
Superblock (256 weights)
 \u251c\u2500\u2500 1\u00d7 FP16 super-scale (d)          \u2190 dequantizes the block scales
 \u251c\u2500\u2500 1\u00d7 FP16 super-minimum (dmin)     \u2190 for asymmetric variants
 \u251c\u2500\u2500 Sub-block 1 (32 weights)
 \u2502   \u251c\u2500\u2500 Quantized scale (4-8 bits)  \u2190 NOT full FP16!
 \u2502   \u251c\u2500\u2500 Quantized minimum
 \u2502   \u2514\u2500\u2500 Weight data (4-bit nibbles)
 \u251c\u2500\u2500 Sub-block 2...
 \u2514\u2500\u2500 Sub-block 8...

The key innovation: the scales themselves are quantized. Instead of storing an FP16 scale per 32 weights (expensive overhead), K-quants store quantized scales with a single FP16 super-scale per 256 weights. It’s like compressing a JPEG, then compressing the compression tables. The “K” in K-quants: the block scales are optimized using K-means clustering, finding the best discrete scale values to minimize total quantization error.

Q4_K_M: The Community Consensus

Q4_K_M became the default recommendation across the local AI community. The “M” stands for “medium” — balanced between aggressive compression and careful preservation. It applies different quantization levels to different tensor types:

Tensor TypeQuant LevelWhy
Embeddings, outputQ6_KToken identity is fragile
Attention value projectionsQ6_KEncodes what to extract
FFN down-projectionsQ6_KFinal information gate
Query projections, FFN gatesQ4_KMore tolerant of rounding

For Llama-3.1-8B, Q4_K_M produces 4.89 bits per weight — 4.58 GiB. It fits comfortably in 8 GB of RAM. This is why Q4_K_M became the default: it preserves quality where the model is sensitive (embeddings, attention values) while compressing aggressively where the model is tolerant (FFN gates, query projections). The bits are allocated based on measured sensitivity, not uniformly. It’s the quantization equivalent of variable bitrate encoding in audio — more bits during complex passages, fewer bits during silence.

Importance Matrices: Tell the Quantizer What Matters

Below 3 bits per weight, block quantization alone isn’t enough. The model starts producing gibberish. At IQ2_XXS (2.38 bits per weight), a naive quantization produces incoherent text. The same model, quantized with an importance matrix, holds together.

The rescue: importance matrices (imatrix). The workflow is straightforward:

# Step 1: Generate importance matrix from calibration data
llama-imatrix -m model-f16.gguf -f calibration.txt -o imatrix.dat

# Step 2: Quantize using the importance matrix
llama-quantize --imatrix imatrix.dat model-f16.gguf model-IQ3_M.gguf IQ3_M

# The imatrix tells the quantizer which weights matter most.
# High-importance: preserved carefully. Low-importance: compressed hard.
# Zero runtime overhead — all work happens at quantization time.

Run the model on representative text using llama-imatrix, record activation statistics — which weights produce large activations, which ones are quiet. Feed that importance matrix to llama-quantize via the --imatrix flag. High-importance weights get more bits. Low-importance weights get fewer. The result has zero inference overhead — all the extra computation happens at quantization time, not at runtime.

Two counterintuitive findings. First, diverse, somewhat random calibration data works better than domain-specific text. If you calibrate on medical journals and then use the model for code generation, the importance matrix is biased. Varied, general-purpose text produces better all-around quantization. Second, the impact scales inversely with bit-width. At Q4_K_M and above, importance matrices offer marginal benefit — there are enough bits to go around. At Q3_K_M, noticeable improvement. At IQ2 and below, essential — the difference between coherent output and word salad.

05 // The Radical Frontier

What if every weight in a neural network was one of three values: -1, 0, or +1? No floating-point multiplication at all. Every matrix multiply becomes pure addition and subtraction. The weight matrix is no longer a dense grid of precise decimal numbers — it’s a sparse instruction set: add this, subtract that, skip the rest.

That sounds like a thought experiment. A parlor trick that couldn’t possibly produce a working language model. Microsoft Research built it. And it works.

BitNet: The Ternary Revolution

The idea traces back to “The Era of 1-bit LLMs” (Ma et al., Microsoft Research, February 2024). The claim was extraordinary: you can train a Transformer where every weight is constrained to {-1, 0, +1} and match FP16 quality at scale. Three possible values means log2(3) = 1.58 bits per weight — the theoretical minimum to encode a ternary value. Hence the name: BitNet b1.58.

The quantization formula is beautiful in its simplicity:

# BitNet b1.58 — absmean quantization
gamma = mean(abs(W))          # average magnitude of weight matrix
W_ternary = round(W / gamma)  # divide by average, round to integer
W_ternary = clamp(W_ternary, -1, +1)  # clamp to {-1, 0, +1}

# Matrix multiplication becomes:
#   +1 \u2192 add the activation
#   -1 \u2192 subtract the activation
#    0 \u2192 skip
# No multiply-accumulate. Just accumulate.

In April 2025, Microsoft released BitNet b1.58 2B4T — the first open-source natively-trained ternary LLM. Two billion parameters. Four trillion training tokens. The numbers are startling:

BitNet b1.58 2B

Qwen 2.5 1.5B

Let that sink in. BitNet b1.58 2B uses less memory than a single high-resolution photograph on your phone. It runs on a CPU at 29 milliseconds per token — human reading speed. It scores within striking distance of models using 6–12x more memory.

Six times less memory. Twice as fast on CPU. Twelve times less energy. And benchmark quality within 2% of Qwen 2.5 1.5B on average (slightly behind on MMLU, slightly ahead on GSM8K and Arc-Challenge). The energy savings on 7nm chips are staggering: 71.4x fewer arithmetic operations for matrix multiplication compared to FP16. This isn’t a marginal improvement. It’s a different category of computation.

The architecture replaces nn.Linear with BitLinear — same Transformer skeleton (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings), but fundamentally different arithmetic. When every weight is ternary, you don’t need a multiply-accumulate unit. Multiply by +1 means add the activation. Multiply by -1 means subtract. Multiply by 0 means skip. Integer addition is orders of magnitude cheaper than floating-point multiplication. The hardware implications cascade: simpler ALUs, less power, less heat, potentially custom silicon that’s dramatically more efficient than general-purpose GPUs.

The catch: BitNet is not post-training quantization. You cannot take Qwen 2.5 72B and “BitNet it.” The model must be trained from scratch with ternary weights. It learns to express knowledge through -1, 0, and +1 during training — it doesn’t get compressed into that representation afterward. This is a fundamentally different training paradigm, and the cost of training from scratch is the reason ternary models are still rare. You also need Microsoft’s dedicated bitnet.cpp runtime with optimized kernels for ternary arithmetic. Standard transformers gives zero efficiency gains — it doesn’t know how to exploit the ternary structure.

On x86 CPUs, bitnet.cpp is 2.37x to 6.17x faster than llama.cpp for equivalent model sizes. On ARM (MacBook), 1.37x to 5.07x faster. Energy reduction: 72–82%. The speedup comes from replacing floating-point multiply-accumulate units with simple integer addition, which is fundamentally cheaper on every processor architecture. The energy savings compound: less compute per operation, fewer operations per inference, less memory bandwidth consumed.

The projection nobody has fulfilled yet: bitnet.cpp can run a 100-billion-parameter ternary model on a single CPU at 5–7 tokens per second. Human reading speed. No GPU. A 100B model — the size of GPT-3 — in roughly 12 GB of RAM, on a laptop. Nobody has trained and released such a model. But the framework is waiting. The question is not whether the hardware can do it. The question is whether anyone will spend the training compute. A 100B model training run costs millions of dollars. The potential payoff is enormous — a model the size of GPT-3 that runs on a laptop, no internet required, no GPU required, at reading speed. But nobody has placed that bet yet.

The implications ripple outward. If ternary models scale to 100B+ and maintain quality, the entire economics of AI inference changes. GPU clusters become less critical. Edge devices become more capable. The power consumption of AI drops by an order of magnitude. Countries without data centers can run frontier models on commodity hardware. The constraints that define who can and cannot deploy AI shift fundamentally. All from replacing multiplication with addition.

The Kernel Lesson

Here is the most important number in this article.

Benchmark: Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct on an NVIDIA H200, via vLLM:

MethodThroughputvs FP16
FP16 baseline461 tok/s1.00x
AWQ (standard kernel)68 tok/s0.15x
GPTQ (standard kernel)277 tok/s0.60x
GPTQ + Marlin712 tok/s1.55x
AWQ + Marlin741 tok/s1.61x

Read that again. AWQ without the Marlin kernel runs at 15% of FP16 speed. The mathematically elegant 4-bit quantization is catastrophically slower than not quantizing at all. How is this possible? The model is four times smaller. Shouldn’t it be faster?

The answer is that quantization doesn’t reduce the amount of computation. It changes the shape of computation. The GPU still performs the same number of multiply-accumulate operations. But now each one requires dequantizing the INT4 weight to FP16 first, computing the multiply, and accumulating. The default dequantization kernel — the code that unpacks 4-bit integers back to floating point — is so unoptimized that this overhead overwhelms the memory bandwidth savings. You saved VRAM but burned clock cycles. A latency nightmare disguised as an optimization.

AWQ with the Marlin kernel: 741 tok/s. 10.9x faster from the kernel alone. The weights didn’t change. The algorithm didn’t change. The GPU didn’t change. Only the execution changed. Marlin interleaves dequantization with tensor core operations so tightly that neither pipeline stalls waiting for the other. It’s the difference between a runner who stops to tie their shoes every hundred meters and one who never breaks stride.

The Kernel Gap: Same Weights, Different Execution

MARLIN (Mixed-precision Auto-Regressive LINear kernel) was published at PPoPP 2025 by the same IST Austria group behind GPTQ — Elias Frantar and Dan Alistarh. It’s an FP16×INT4 matrix multiplication kernel that takes 4-bit quantized weights and FP16 activations and multiplies them with near-ideal efficiency.

Three techniques make it work. First, asynchronous weight loading: Marlin uses cuda::memcpy_async (Ampere and newer) to load INT4 weights directly to shared memory without blocking compute. A cache eviction policy prevents polluting the L2 with weights that are never reused, preserving L2 space for activations that are. Second, circular shared-memory double buffering: while the GPU computes on buffer A, it loads the next tile into buffer B. No synchronization stalls. The current iteration’s compute uses operands fetched to registers in the previous iteration. Third, Stream-K parallelization: non-uniform work partitioning along the reduction dimension that minimizes idle SMs when work doesn’t divide evenly.

The result: near-ideal 3.87x speedup (theoretical maximum for 4-bit: 16/4 = 4x, minus group scaling overhead) sustained across batch sizes 1–32. Prior work hit ~4x only at batch_size=1 and degraded rapidly. As the batch size grows, the operation becomes compute-bound rather than memory-bound, and the advantage of smaller weights diminishes. Marlin maintains the speedup up to batch 64–128 before this crossover takes over. End-to-end in vLLM on real serving workloads: ~2.8x speedup. Not 4x, because a production serving system includes attention, KV cache management, scheduling overhead, and other components that aren’t weight-matrix multiplies. But 2.8x on end-to-end latency is enormous for a technique that’s invisible to the user.

The vLLM issue tracker (vllm-project/vllm#2268) tells the story plainly. Before Marlin, the team acknowledged: “AWQ int4 GEMM kernel was too slow — fp16 faster than int4 in every batchsize.” This was a known issue, not a bug. The default kernel was simply not designed for mixed-precision arithmetic. Marlin was the fix. And it came from the same IST Austria group that built GPTQ — the same researchers who understood both the math and the metal.

The lesson

The algorithm is not the bottleneck — the execution is. AWQ’s math is fine. GPTQ’s math is fine. But without a kernel that can deliver the theoretical throughput of 4-bit weights, you’re leaving performance on the table — or worse, you’re slower than not quantizing at all. The vLLM issue tracker explicitly acknowledged this: “AWQ int4 GEMM kernel was too slow — fp16 faster than int4 in every batchsize.”

KVQuant: The Other Memory Wall

There’s one more frontier, and it targets a different bottleneck entirely. At long context lengths, the KV cache — not the model weights — dominates memory. Every token you process adds a key vector and a value vector to the cache, for every layer, for every attention head. A 7B model with a million-token context can require hundreds of GB just for cached keys and values. You’ve quantized the weights to 4 bits. The model fits. And then the cache fills up and you’re out of memory anyway.

KVQuant (Hooper, Kim, et al., NeurIPS 2024) compresses the KV cache to 3 bits with less than 0.1 perplexity degradation. It combines four techniques: per-channel key quantization (different scaling per channel, not per token), pre-RoPE key quantization (quantize before rotary position embedding to avoid distribution distortion), non-uniform quantization that considers sensitivity rather than just magnitude, and per-vector dense-and-sparse decomposition that explicitly preserves heavy-tailed outliers.

The result: a 7B model holding one million tokens of context on a single A100-80GB. On 8x A100s, ten million tokens. Custom CUDA kernels achieve ~1.7x speedup over FP16 matrix-vector multiply for attention computation.

Weight quantization shrinks the model. KV cache quantization extends the context. They’re orthogonal — you can stack them. A GPTQ-quantized model with KVQuant cache gives you both a smaller model and a longer context window simultaneously. The weight quantization frees memory that the KV cache then fills with context. You’re not just saving space — you’re reallocating it from a place where precision doesn’t matter (weight storage) to a place where capacity does (context memory).

This bridges directly to the serving story in Part III, where context length is often the binding constraint. As models push toward million-token context windows, and users expect multi-turn conversations that accumulate thousands of tokens of history, the KV cache becomes the dominant memory consumer. Weight quantization got the model into the GPU. KV cache quantization keeps it from filling up as the conversation grows.

06 // The Spectrum

Quantization is not a setting you toggle. It’s a spectrum of techniques, each targeting a different bottleneck in the compute stack. The methods in this article operate at five different levels, and a fully optimized deployment combines them.

Interactive: The Quantization Taxonomy

Weight-only PTQ (GPTQ, AWQ, GGML) is the most common and easiest to apply. Download a pre-quantized model from Hugging Face, run it with Ollama or LM Studio. No calibration data, no training, no expertise required. This is what 90% of local AI users encounter, and Q4_K_M is the community default.

Weight + activation quantization (SmoothQuant, LLM.int8()) enables full INT8 acceleration — both operands in INT8, unlocking INT8 tensor cores and integer GEMM kernels. This is critical for serving workloads where throughput matters more than download size. If you’re running a single model for your own use, weight-only quantization is fine. If you’re serving thousands of concurrent users, you need both sides quantized to hit peak hardware utilization.

Train-time quantization (BitNet) is the most radical approach: highest reward, highest cost. You get 1.58-bit weights with zero post-training quality loss, but you must train from scratch. The barrier isn’t technical — the framework exists. It’s economic: training a 100B model from scratch costs millions of dollars in compute.

KV cache quantization (KVQuant) is orthogonal to everything above. It targets a completely different memory pool, extending the context window rather than shrinking the model. As context lengths push toward millions of tokens, KV cache compression will become as essential as weight compression is today.

And runtime kernels (Marlin, bitnet.cpp, ExLlama) are the execution layer that makes everything else actually fast. The most elegant quantization algorithm in the world is useless — or actively harmful — without a kernel that can deliver the theoretical throughput. This is the lesson that the AWQ/Marlin comparison hammers home: math is necessary but not sufficient. Execution is everything.

A fully optimized serving stack combines methods from multiple layers. AWQ weights + KVQuant cache + Marlin kernel gives you a smaller model, a longer context window, and faster wall-clock execution. Each technique targets a different bottleneck. Together, they compound.

This is the view from early 2026. The quantization field has matured from “can we make it work at all?” to “how do we optimize across the full stack?” The research frontier has shifted to training-time quantization (BitNet and its successors), sub-2-bit representations, and hardware-aware co-design where the chip architecture is built around the quantization scheme rather than the other way around. The question is no longer whether models can be compressed. It’s how much intelligence you can pack into the fewest possible bits, on the cheapest possible hardware, at the lowest possible energy cost.

The practical rule of thumb for local inference: if your model fits in VRAM at Q4_K_M, use Q4_K_M. If it doesn’t fit, step down to Q3_K_M or IQ3_M with an imatrix — but know you’re trading quality. For serving deployments, the calculus is different. You care about throughput (tokens per second per dollar), not just whether the model fits. GPTQ and AWQ with Marlin kernels are the standard. SmoothQuant for W8A8 when you want maximum hardware utilization. KVQuant when context length is the binding constraint. And the emerging BitNet paradigm for dedicated hardware — once someone trains and releases a 70B+ ternary model, the economics of serving could shift dramatically.

What Changed

What Changed

Zoom out. In August 2022, running a 175B-parameter model required multiple A100 GPUs and cost thousands of dollars per hour. Quantization was a niche topic that the mainstream ML community considered boring plumbing — “nobody cares about printers.” The most cited AI papers were about scaling laws, training techniques, and architecture innovations. Nobody was writing breathless blog posts about number formats.

By March 2023, a single person had a 7B model running on a MacBook. The barrier between “lab capability” and “your laptop” had been shattered. By late 2023, every serious open-source model shipped with GGUF quantizations on day one. Quantization wasn’t an afterthought — it was part of the release pipeline. By 2024, the community had standardized on Q4_K_M as the default, AWQ had won the MLSys Best Paper, Marlin had shown that kernel design matters as much as algorithm design, and KVQuant had extended the context window from thousands to millions of tokens. By 2025, Microsoft had trained a ternary model from scratch and the inference framework was ready for 100B parameters on a CPU.

The timeline spans only 30 months. From “can we do INT8 without breaking things?” to “every weight is -1, 0, or +1 and the model still works.” From academic papers with modest ambitions to infrastructure running on millions of devices. From Tim Dettmers discovering outlier features in a lab to Georgi Gerganov’s code powering Ollama on your phone. The revolution was quiet. It happened in C code, in quantization tables, in CUDA kernels. Nobody noticed until their laptop started talking back.

The field moved from “nobody cares about printers” to “this is THE enabling technology for running AI on consumer hardware” in under three years. A 72B model that needed two A100s now fits on a gaming PC. A 7B model that needed a GPU now runs on a phone. A 2B ternary model fits in less memory than a photo and runs at reading speed on a CPU.

The intelligence didn’t change. The precision did. And it turned out the intelligence was never in the precision. It was in the patterns, the directions, the relative relationships between weights. The 14 extra bits per weight we’d been storing? Noise. The model knew all along that it didn’t need them. We just hadn’t asked.

Looking forward

A 72B model at 4 bits fits on a gaming PC. Problem solved? Only if you’re serving one user. Put ten thousand users on the same GPU cluster and a new problem emerges: memory fragmentation, request scheduling, prefix sharing. The model fits in memory. Now you need to make it serve a crowd. Part III enters the server room.
