// 01 — The Frame Problem

SEGMENT → TRACK → DETECT → READ → UNDERSTAND → GROUND

Thirty images per second. Your brain sees one continuous story. The machine sees thirty strangers. The gap between them is the entire problem._

A child watches a dog run behind a tree. She waits. The dog appears on the other side. She is not surprised. She has a model of the world that persists through occlusion — she knows a disappearing dog is not a vanishing dog. She is four years old, and she solves the hardest open problem in computer vision before breakfast.

Machines cannot do this. Feed a state-of-the-art image classifier every frame of that video and it will label each one independently: dog, tree, grass, tree, grass, dog. It does not know the dog in frame 47 is the same dog from frame 12. Every frame is a new universe. No memory. No continuity. No time.

The flipbook below makes this visceral. Click the frames to “play” the sequence — your eye interpolates motion from the colored shapes. Now look at any single frame. Just rectangles. The motion is in your head, not in the data:

click a frame, or drag across to flip through — motion only exists between frames

Drag across those frames fast enough and you see a green dot running, ducking behind a tree, emerging on the other side. Stop on any single frame and the dot has no past, no trajectory, no identity. That gap — between thirty frozen images and one continuous scene — is the entire problem.

Three capabilities bridge it:

Video segmentation gives you pixel-level masks that persist through time. Not “there is a person” but “this is Person #3, the same one from 40 seconds ago, and here is her exact silhouette at every frame in between.”

Video OCR reads text that moves, blurs, rotates, and compresses. A license plate crossing 200 frames. A ticker scrolling across a broadcast. Subtitle extraction from a language you don’t speak.

Vision-language models connect sight to speech. “What happened between minute 3 and minute 7?” — answered not from a transcript, but from watching the video.

Three questions follow: How do you keep a mask alive across 10,000 frames? How do you read the text that moves with those objects? And how do you ask a language model to watch the whole thing and tell you what happened? Start with masks — because everything that follows collapses if the mask is wrong.

// 02 — Masks in Motion

SEGMENT → TRACK → DETECT → READ → UNDERSTAND → GROUND

A perfect mask on frame 0. A compounding disaster by frame 500. Understanding why tells you everything about what has to be built._

Image segmentation labels every pixel in a photograph. Solved. Then you press play, and everything breaks.

Think of it as a stencil. You cut a perfect silhouette of a person from cardboard — pixel-precise, every curve of their outline. You hold it over the next frame. The person shifted two pixels to the right. Raised an arm. The stencil doesn’t fit. You re-cut it. By frame 500, after 500 re-cuts, each slightly wrong, errors have compounded into a silhouette that fits someone else entirely.

This is the mask propagation problem. And it gets worse in three ways:

IoU — Intersection over Union

The standard mask quality metric. Draw both the predicted mask and the ground-truth mask; divide the overlapping area by the total area covered by either. Perfect prediction = 1.0. No overlap = 0. Above 0.5 means the prediction covers the right region; above 0.8 is production-quality.

J&F — Jaccard & F-measure

J (Jaccard / IoU): region accuracy — how well the mask pixels overlap the ground truth. F: contour accuracy — how well the mask edges follow the object boundary. J&F is their average, reported on a 0–100 scale. Random guessing: ~5–10. Pre-2019 CNN baselines: ~50–60. 2024 SOTA: ~86–92. Human consistency: ~95+. These numbers reappear in every benchmark table in this article.

Semantic — class only

Every pixel gets a label: road, sky, person. No individual identity. Two people are one magenta blob. Easiest to extend to video — labels just need to stay consistent across frames.

Instance — class + identity

Every pixel gets a label and an ID: Person #1, Person #2. In video, this means maintaining identity through occlusion, appearance change, and camera motion. Person #2 must remain Person #2 for the entire clip. This is the hard problem.

Panoptic — everything at once

Background stuff (road, sky) gets semantic labels. Foreground things (cars, people) get instance IDs. All of it, consistently through time. The 2024 PVUW winner: 58.26 VPQ on VIPSeg. LSVOS 2025 pushed MOSE to 86.37 J&F.

// 03 — The Memory Bank

SEGMENT → TRACK → DETECT → READ → UNDERSTAND → GROUND
Three terms that matter in this chapter

ConvLSTM: a type of recurrent network that applies convolution at each time step. It processes frames in sequence, updating a single hidden vector. The vector can only grow so large — information about older frames gets progressively overwritten.
GRU hidden state: a compact “summary” vector a recurrent network carries forward. It has no index — you can’t reach back and ask “what was in state 47?”
Key-value pair: like a dictionary entry. The “key” is a compact descriptor (what this frame looked like). The “value” is the full feature representation. A query from the current frame can look up any past key and retrieve its value directly — no sequential scan required.

Memory is not storage. The guard who takes too many notes is as lost as the one who takes none. XMem found the right balance — and it changed the field._

Before 2019, video segmentation used recurrent networks. A ConvLSTM compressed everything the model had seen into a single vector. By frame 200, the vector had drifted so far from reality that the model was segmenting ghosts.

Then Space-Time Memory Networks arrived (Oh et al., ICCV 2019). The insight: don’t compress — remember. Store every past frame as an explicit key-value pair. Let the current frame query the memory like a database lookup. Frame 300 can directly access frame 1 without passing through 299 intermediate states.

It worked beautifully. For about 500 frames. Then GPU memory exploded.

XMem’s answer came from psychology. Imagine a security guard watching 16 camera feeds. Three kinds of memory keep her effective:

Sensory memory is what’s on screen right now. Everything, all at once, gone the moment the next frame arrives. A GRU hidden state, updated every single frame.

Working memory is the notepad where she jots “red jacket guy entered at 2:14 PM.” A handful of recent, high-detail key-value pairs, stored every r frames. Capacity: Tmax slots.

Long-term memory is experience: “that delivery driver comes every Tuesday.” Compressed, low-detail, but persistent across the entire shift. When working memory fills up, a potentiation algorithm consolidates the most informative entries into long-term storage. Everything else is forgotten.

That tradeoff — temporal resolution versus memory budget — is the central tension of every video segmentation system since 2019. XMem (ECCV 2022) processes 10,000-frame videos on a single GPU. STM crashes at 500. Cutie (CVPR 2024) added top-down object queries that prevent the guard from confusing two people in similar jackets — +8.7 J&F on the hard MOSE benchmark.

Trivia: Why STM crashed at 500 frames

The math: a 240×160 feature map at 512 channels per frame × 500 frames = 9.6 GB just for the memory bank, before any computation. Every new frame added another 20 MB of key-value pairs with no eviction policy. XMem bounded this to ~1 GB regardless of video length by the consolidation mechanism the interactive demonstrates. The guard’s notepad has a fixed number of pages — that constraint is the entire innovation.

But the biggest leap didn’t come from a better memory. It came from a model that learned to speak.

// 04 — SAM 3

SEGMENT → TRACK → DETECT → READ → UNDERSTAND → GROUND

SAM 2 needed a click. SAM 3 needs a sentence. One change collapses three separate models into one._

SAM changed image segmentation in 2023. Click anywhere, get a mask. SAM 2 did it for video in 2024. Click once, track through occlusion, at 44 FPS. But both had a limitation: you had to point at the thing you wanted. You needed to know where it was before the model could help you find it.

SAM 3 accepts text. “Yellow school bus.” “The person holding a coffee cup.” No click required. The model finds it, segments it, and tracks it through the entire video. Detection, segmentation, and tracking — unified.

SAM 1 was a magnifying glass — you point, it shows you what’s there. SAM 2 was a magnifying glass on a track — you point once, it follows. SAM 3 is a search dog. You say “find the yellow bus” and it goes. No pointing. No coordinates. Just language.

The leap is not incremental. It collapses the Grounding DINO + SAM 2 pipeline into a single forward pass. Previously you needed one model to understand the text (“yellow school bus”), another to find it in the image, and a third to segment and track it. SAM 3 does all three.

From SAM 2 to SAM 3

Image encoder: Hiera (hierarchical ViT), same as SAM 2 but larger variants.

Text encoder: new — accepts open-vocabulary noun phrases. Trained on SA-Co dataset: 214K unique phrases across 126K images and videos (50× more concepts than COCO/LVIS).

Memory bank: inherited from SAM 2 — FIFO frame memory + object pointer memory.

Occlusion head: inherited — suppresses mask when target is hidden, continues writing to memory.

SAM 3.1 (March 2026): Object Multiplexing — processes up to 16 objects simultaneously in a single forward pass. 7× faster inference at high object counts. 32 FPS on a single H100 for medium-density scenes.

The SA-Co Dataset’s Strange Decision

Meta built SA-Co — SAM 3’s training dataset — entirely from licensed video sources. No internet-scraped images. The 214K unique concept phrases were enumerated not by human annotators but by a GPT-4-class language model parsing object descriptions from video captions.

The result: SAM 3 can segment “the device that converts motion to electricity” (a generator) but struggles with culturally specific objects not well-represented in licensed stock footage. The bet was that licensed video gives better spatial diversity than web scraping — and the benchmark numbers suggest it paid off.

You have masks. Identity. Temporal persistence. Text-based prompting. The architecture that will connect language to segmentation at a fundamental level is still two chapters away. First, you need to understand what language models see when they watch video — and why even a million-token context window is not enough.

The segmentation pipeline now tracks objects with the same key-value memory architecture that gives XMem its bounded-GPU guarantee. The OCR pipeline uses the same structural idea differently: each character across multiple frames is a vote to be aggregated. Both tracking problems — visual identity across frames, textual identity across frames — share the same underlying solution. Now the question shifts: what are the best tools for the text side of that problem?

// 05 — Moving Text

SEGMENT → TRACK → DETECT → READ → UNDERSTAND → GROUND

OCR on a photograph: solved. OCR on video: different. Video gives you something no image can — multiple attempts at the same text._

OCR on a photograph is a solved problem. Tesseract reads a clean page at 99%. But OCR on video is a different animal. The text moves. The camera shakes. H.264 smears character edges into blocking artifacts. A license plate perfectly legible in frame 47 is a motion-blurred streak in frame 48.

And yet video gives you something no single image can: multiple attempts at the same text.

Think of it as a police lineup with multiple witnesses. One witness says the license plate started with “A8C.” Another says “ABC.” A third is sure it was “ABC.” You weight by confidence and the truth emerges. Each frame is a witness. Temporal redundancy is the gift.

Trivia: Why license plates failed in the UK until 2019

UK plates use a modified Charles Wright font designed specifically to defeat automatic plate readers. The font introduces deliberate ambiguities between 0/O and 1/I that human readers resolve from context (“a plate number won’t start with OO”). Neural OCR eventually beat it not by learning the font — but by voting across enough frames that even ambiguous glyphs resolved. The temporal redundancy of video broke a 30-year font-based defense.

Confidence voting works because video gives you redundancy. Eight frames, eight witnesses, one truth. But voting only recovers the text — it doesn’t tell you which OCR engine to vote with, how to handle math formulas, or what to do when one frame shows vertical Japanese and the next shows handwritten English. The tool you reach for depends on what you’re trying to read.

// 06 — The OCR Arsenal

SEGMENT → TRACK → DETECT → READ → UNDERSTAND → GROUND

Four systems, four different bets on how to read. Which one survives temporal aggregation?_

Confidence voting solved the temporal problem. Now the question is which reader to vote with.

The OCR pipeline from Ch. 5 has a RECOGNIZE stage with a blank inside: CRNN? SVTR? GOT-OCR? It depends on what the text looks like. A license plate is not a math formula. A subtitle ticker is not a handwritten receipt. Four systems made different bets on how to fill that blank — and one of them integrates temporal aggregation directly.

Four Bets

Every production OCR system places a bet on how reading should work:

PaddleOCR (PP-OCRv5): the assembly line. Detect text regions, then recognize characters in each region. Two specialized models, each optimized for its stage. 370+ chars/sec on CPU. The temporal-aggregation upgrade (PaddleOCR-VL 1.5) bolts a 0.9B VLM onto the pipeline for end-to-end document understanding.

GOT-OCR 2.0: the polyglot. One 580M model reads everything — plain text, math formulas, molecular formulas, tables, charts, sheet music. No pipeline stages, no separate detector. The bet: a single end-to-end model is better than a two-stage pipeline if the model is good enough.

EasyOCR: the baseline. CRAFT detects character-level regions, CRNN recognizes them. Simple, deployable, 80+ languages. No temporal integration — frame-by-frame only. The system you start with before you know what you need.

TrOCR: the specialist. Recognition only (no detection). SOTA on handwriting and receipts. Must be paired with a separate detector. The field has moved past single-task transformers toward unified VLM-based systems, but TrOCR remains unmatched on its niche.

Question: which of these was designed to aggregate across video frames? Answer: none of the four, originally. Only PaddleOCR-VL 1.5 (2026) integrates temporal context natively. For the others, you need the voting pipeline from Ch. 5.

CTC Was Designed for Speech, Not Text

CRNN’s secret weapon — CTC loss — was invented for speech recognition (Graves et al., 2006). It handles variable-length outputs without needing character-aligned training data. This turns out to be exactly why it works so well on license plates: the character widths vary, the spacing is irregular, and CTC doesn’t care. It maps the whole feature sequence to a string without knowing where one character ends and another begins.

Visual Rhythm

A 2025 ALPR technique: compress the entire video to a single Visual Rhythm image — take one horizontal line of pixels from the plate region across all frames and stack them vertically. Run OCR once on the composite. No tracking. No per-frame detection. One pass. Accuracy improves because the stacking averages out per-frame noise. It’s confidence voting without the voting — the compression does it geometrically.

// 07 — The Token Wall

How an image becomes tokens

A Vision Transformer (ViT) chops an image into a grid of small patches — say, a 336×336 image into a 24×24 grid of 14px patches — giving 576 patches. Each patch is converted to a fixed-size vector. That vector is a token. CLIP ViT-L at 336px resolution produces exactly 576 tokens per frame. These visual tokens are what the language model reads. More resolution = more patches = finer spatial detail, but exponentially more context window usage.

One minute of video. One million tokens. The context window is a suitcase. Every VLM is a packing strategy._

One minute of video at 30 fps. 1,800 frames. A CLIP ViT-L at 336px produces 576 tokens per frame. Multiply: 1,036,800 tokens. For one minute. No context window holds that.

Think of it as packing a suitcase for a trip. The suitcase is your context window. The clothes are frames. You can either bring fewer outfits (sample fewer frames), fold tighter (compress tokens per frame), or buy a bigger suitcase (expand context). Every VLM is a packing strategy.

// 08 — How VLMs Watch

The context window holds the tokens. It does not hold the understanding. Long context ≠ long memory._

Three people are watching the same two-hour film. One speed-reads the whole thing but misses details. One annotates every frame with colored bookmarks that encode where and when. One has a photographic memory and just ingests the entire reel. Three architectures. Three bets on the same wall.

Three People, Three Strategies

Map each person from the drop-cap to a real architecture:

The Speed-Reader = Gemini 2.5 Flash. Ingests the native video stream, processes at $0.30/M tokens. Fast, cheap, covers the whole film. Misses subtle details in dense scenes because it skims.

The Annotator = Qwen3-VL. Encodes every frame with three-axis bookmarks — when (temporal), where vertically (height), where horizontally (width) — using MRoPE (Multimodal Rotary Position Embedding). Can pinpoint events to the second in a 2-hour video. 99.5% accuracy on inserted-frame detection. The most precise, but the most compute-hungry per frame.

The Photographic Memory = Gemini 2.5 Pro. 1M token context window. Native video stream + audio. Holds ~55 minutes in a single call. SOTA at 85.2% Video-MME. The biggest suitcase — but even it can’t hold a 2-hour film at full resolution.

Fourth player: GPT-5 — the Multimodal Reasoner. Still frame-based (no native video stream). Best on shorter clips (<5 min). ~84% Video-MME. Most expensive at ~$0.065/min.

The consumer sweet spot (2026)

Qwen3-VL-7B int4 on an RTX 4060 Ti (16 GB). At 1fps it processes a 30-minute video in ~12 minutes. Video-MME accuracy ~73% — 12 points behind Gemini 2.5 Pro, zero API cost. For most use cases (content moderation, video search, accessibility captions), that 12-point gap doesn’t matter.

MRoPE explained: standard position encoding gives each token one number (position 1, 2, 3…). MRoPE gives each token three independent coordinates — temporal (which second), height (row in frame), width (column in frame). The axes are encoded separately using rotary embeddings, so the model can distinguish “same place, different time” from “different place, same time.”

Why 85% Is Not As Good As It Sounds

Video-MME v1 (2024) was saturating. Multiple-choice format, limited question diversity, 25% random-guess baseline. When Gemini 2.5 Pro hit 85.2%, it was within spitting distance of human performance on the easy questions.

Video-MME-v2 (April 2026) changed the game: 3,300+ hours of human annotation, tri-level progressive hierarchy, and Non-Linear Scoring that penalizes random guessing. On this benchmark, Gemini 3 Pro leads at 49.4 NL — far from human expert performance. The old benchmark said “almost there.” The new one says “nowhere close.”

The 14.8% long-video accuracy drop is the other sobering number. Even Gemini 2.5 Pro degrades from short to long videos. The context window holds the tokens, but the attention mechanism dilutes relevant information over a million positions. Long context ≠ long understanding.

What attention does, in one sentence

For each token, attention computes a weighted average of every other token in the context window — weighting by relevance. The cost is quadratic: 1M tokens means 1 trillion relevance scores. Worse, because all weights sum to 1, relevant tokens get diluted as the window grows. Finding the right needle in a million-token haystack is the fundamental bottleneck.

// 09 — Grounded Understanding

SEGMENT → TRACK → DETECT → READ → UNDERSTAND → GROUND

A segmentation model cannot reason. A language model cannot draw a mask. LISA does both with one token._

“Segment the thing that makes noise when the wind blows.” No bounding box. No class label. Just a sentence that requires reasoning. A segmentation model cannot do this — it doesn’t know what makes noise. A VLM cannot do this — it reasons but cannot draw a mask. LISA does both.

LISA: The <SEG> Token

What is a “hidden embedding”?

Inside a transformer, every token becomes a vector at each layer. These intermediate vectors are called hidden states or hidden embeddings. For the <SEG> token, the final hidden embedding encodes everything the model “understood” about what should be segmented. It is not text — it is a ~4096-dimensional float vector rich in geometric and semantic meaning. LISA routes this vector to a SAM decoder that was trained to turn it into a pixel mask.

LISA (CVPR 2024 Oral) adds one token to the LLM vocabulary: <SEG>. When the model generates it, the token’s hidden embedding is fed to a SAM-style mask decoder. Language reasoning in, pixel mask out. One forward pass.

The elegance is in what it doesn’t need. No bounding box. No class vocabulary. No multi-stage pipeline. The LLM reasons, emits <SEG>, the decoder draws. GLaMM (CVPR 2024) extended this to grounded conversations — every object mentioned in text gets its own mask. “One Token to Seg Them All” (NeurIPS 2024) extended it to video.

Meanwhile, SAM 3 made the opposite move: instead of teaching a language model to segment, it taught a segmentation model to understand language. The <SEG> token and SAM 3’s text encoder are converging from opposite directions toward the same destination: models that see, speak, and point simultaneously.

AUSM — Autoregressive Video Segmentation (CVPR 2026)

NVIDIA’s AUSM recasts video segmentation as sequential mask prediction — language-model style, token by token. Built on state-space models (a class of sequence models with fixed-size hidden states, like Mamba) with a spatial state that scales to arbitrary-length video streams. A single architecture for both prompted and unprompted segmentation. 2.5× faster training. If LISA taught a language model to segment, AUSM taught a segmentation model to think like a language model. The convergence is coming from both directions.

The convergence thesis

Five years ago, segmentation and language were separate fields with separate conferences. LISA, GLaMM, SAM 3, AUSM — the boundaries are dissolving. The language model is the segmentation model, because understanding what to segment requires understanding language, and expressing what you found requires generating it. The frame problem was never about pixels. It was about meaning.

Trivia: LISA trained on 239 images

Not 239 thousand. 239. The full ReasonSeg training set contains 239 image-instruction pairs for the complex reasoning category. LISA achieves SOTA on complex reasoning segmentation from 239 examples because the SAM decoder was already pre-trained on 11 million masks — the LLM only has to learn when to emit <SEG>, not how to draw. The heaviest lifting was already done. The <SEG> token is a thin bridge between two pre-trained giants.

Index the video once. Query it infinitely. The indexing pass runs OCR, ASR, VLM captioning, and SAM 3 object detection — everything goes into a vector store with timestamps. At query time, retrieve the relevant segments and run VLM inference only on those. Cost per query drops from dollars to cents.

The Horizon

The child watching the dog run behind the tree still does it better. She has a world model no architecture reproduces. But the machines are getting close. SAM 3 tracks through occlusion using language. Qwen3-VL watches 2-hour videos with second-level precision. LISA draws a mask around “the thing that makes noise in the wind.” AUSM treats segmentation as autoregressive prediction — the same paradigm that unlocked language.

The frame problem — that gap between thirty frozen images and one continuous understanding — has not been solved. But the gap is narrower than it has ever been. The machines still lose identity when lighting changes. They still miss the license plate at motion-blur frame 48. They still drop 15% accuracy in the final 30 minutes of a long video. Each limitation is a research paper waiting to happen. And for the first time, the systems that see, the systems that read, and the systems that reason are becoming the same system.

The map is drawn. The pipeline is wired. What you build on top of it is the next chapter. _
