CH 01 · ACT IThe Missing Gradient

STATE → ACTION → REWARD → STATE′   ← the only diagram in ML that loops
No labels. No dataset. Just a scalar that shows up late and never explains itself.
This chapter names the problem every algorithm in this article is a workaround for._

Supervised learning dies the moment the label disappears. Show a classifier a photo, it guesses “cat,” you say “wrong, it's a fox,” and a gradient flows straight from that correction into every weight that caused the mistake. The signal is instructive — it doesn't just grade the answer, it hands you the right one. Reinforcement learning gets none of that. An agent looks at a position, picks a move, and hears nothing. It picks another, hears nothing. It plays roughly forty moves of chess — forty separate forks where a different choice was available — and then the game ends and exactly one bit of information arrives: win, or lose. Nobody tells it which of the forty moves was the blunder.

That problem has a name and a date. Marvin Minsky wrote it down in 1961, in Steps Toward Artificial Intelligence (Proceedings of the IRE, vol. 49), the paper that first named the credit assignment problem: “In playing a complex game such as chess or checkers … one has a definite success criterion — the game is won or lost. But in the course of play, each ultimate success (or failure) is associated with a vast number of internal decisions. If the run is successful, how can we assign credit for the success … among the multitude of decisions?” Minsky then said the quiet part out loud, and it's worth sitting with: “It is extremely doubtful whether there is enough information in ‘win, lose or draw’ … to permit any learning at all over available time scales.” One scalar. Forty decisions. Nothing in that scalar says which decision it's about.

Why You Can't Backprop Through the World

In supervised learning the loss function is math you wrote, so its derivative exists and you can chase it backward through every layer — ∂loss/∂weights, computed exactly, all the way to the first matrix. In reinforcement learning, the thing that produces your outcome is the world: a chess opponent's decisions, a physics engine's collision code, a customer who clicks or doesn't. You don't own that function. Most of the time it isn't differentiable at all — it's a discrete branch, a human's call, a coin flip nobody logged. There is no ∂(you lost)/∂(move seventeen) to compute, because “you lost” was never a differentiable function of move seventeen in the first place. All you get is a number, and it arrives late.

Every learning system needs a teacher of some kind. The question is what the teacher is allowed to tell you.

Instructive (supervised)

Tells you the correct answer, independent of what you did. “It should have been ‘Paris.’” The signal exists whether you got it right or wrong, and it points straight at the fix.

Evaluative (reinforcement)

Tells you only how good what you did was — a score, not a correction. It says nothing about what the best move would have been, or whether a better one even existed.

That distinction is the whole field in one sentence. A label says here is truth. A reward says here is a number; you figure out what produced it. Every algorithm in this article — from a four-armed slot machine to GRPO training a reasoning model — is a different strategy for squeezing information backward out of a signal that was never designed to be informative about any single decision.

RL Before the Name

Eighteen years before Minsky named the problem, Arthur Samuel was already building a system that lived inside it. In 1959, at IBM, Samuel published Some Studies in Machine Learning Using the Game of Checkers (IBM Journal of Research and Development, vol. 3) — the paper that coined the term “machine learning” itself. His checkers program played games against a copy of itself, adjusted an evaluation function based on how positions turned out, and got measurably better. No labeled dataset of “correct” checkers moves existed to train on — there was only the final result of each game, filtered backward into which board evaluations to trust more.

Given just the rules of checkers, a sense of which side was ahead, and a redundant, incomplete list of position features, Samuel's program needed only 8 to 10 hours of self-play to start beating the person who wrote it. Self-play, plus an evaluation function, plus credit assignment by trial and error — the shape of reinforcement learning, running on a machine two years before the problem it was solving even had a name.

One Number, Two Jobs

A reward has to do double duty: it grades the outcome and it's the only material you have for figuring out which decisions deserve credit. A label never has to do the second job — the correction already points at the fix. That's why reward is a strictly harder signal to learn from than a label, not just a differently-shaped one.

The Loop With No Teacher

Strip away the specifics — chess, checkers, whatever — and every reinforcement learning problem collapses to the same exchange, repeated forever. An agent looks at a state: the board, the pixels, the sensor reading. It picks an action. The environment — everything outside the agent's own head — responds with a new state and a reward, a single number grading what just happened. The agent looks at the new state and does it again. There's no dataset sitting off to the side; the agent's own actions generate the only data it will ever see, and each action changes what data it sees next. That's the diagram below, and it's the only loop in this article worth memorizing on sight — it returns, fully annotated, in the article's last chapter.

Notice what's missing from that loop: no correct-answer arrow, nowhere for a label to live. Reward is the only thing flowing from environment back to agent, and reward alone has to explain forty moves of chess with one bit. The next chapter makes that concrete by throwing away everything except the loop's smallest possible shape — one state that never changes, a handful of actions, and a reward that arrives immediately instead of forty moves later. If credit assignment is still hard when there's nothing left to assign credit across, you'll know the problem was never really about chess. It was always about the number.

CH 02 · ACT IThe Casino

STATE → ACTION → REWARD → STATE′   ← one room, four levers, the state never moves
Four slot machines. Unknown payouts. A hundred pulls, and every one you spend on a losing arm is gone for good.
Explore too little and you never find the best machine. Explore too long and you never cash in on it._

Every pull spends something you can't get back. Four slot machines sit in front of you, each with a fixed but unknown probability of paying out. You get a hundred pulls, total, and that's it. Pull the machine you already trust, and you bank the expected reward but learn nothing new. Pull one you've barely tried, and you might discover it's the best machine in the room — or waste a pull confirming it's the worst. No pull does both jobs. Every single one, you're trading money you can see against information you don't have yet.

This is the multi-armed bandit problem, named for a row of one-armed slot machines — and it's reinforcement learning with everything except the hard part removed. One state. It never changes. Pull an arm, get a reward, pull again from the exact same situation. No sequence of decisions to untangle, no forty-move chess game to credit-assign across. And the problem is still open-ended enough to have generated decades of research, because explore/exploit doesn't go away just because you deleted the state transitions. It's the pure form of the tension every algorithm in this article eventually has to resolve: act on what you know, or spend a turn finding out more.

Regret, Defined Honestly

The metric the field uses to score a bandit strategy is regret: the gap between what you actually earned and what you would have earned by pulling the single best arm every single time. Formally, regret after T pulls is T·μ* − Σ E[reward_t], where μ* is the true mean of the best arm. Notice what that requires: you need to know μ*, the best arm's real payout rate, to compute it — something no algorithm running the casino floor is ever allowed to see. Regret is a research tool, not a runtime signal. You compute it afterward, in simulation, measuring an algorithm against a god's-eye view of the machines it never got to look inside.

Every strategy below is really just a different theory of when to stop trusting your own estimate and go check.

A/B Testing Is a Bandit With ε=1

A standard A/B test splits traffic 50/50 for the full duration of the experiment, then picks a winner at the end. That's ε-greedy with ε fixed at 1 — pure exploration, zero exploitation, for the entire budget. Every visitor routed to the losing variant during the test is a pull your casino strategy would have stopped making by hour two. Bandit-based experimentation exists specifically to stop paying that tax.

Picture each arm's true payout rate as hiding inside a shrinking window of doubt. The first time you pull an arm, the window is enormous — you have one data point, and it could be a fluke in either direction. UCB1 doesn't score the arm by its average alone; it scores the arm by the top edge of that window — average plus a bonus sized to how uncertain you still are. Pull an arm more, and the window tightens, the bonus shrinks, and the score converges on the truth. An arm you've never tried has an effectively infinite bonus — UCB1 always tries every arm at least once before it trusts an average over a hunch.

The Oldest Algorithm in This Article

Thompson sampling predates the term “reinforcement learning,” predates the term “multi-armed bandit,” and predates Minsky's credit assignment paper by 28 years. William R. Thompson, in the Department of Pathology at Yale, published On the Likelihood that One Unknown Probability Exceeds Another in View of the Evidence of Two Samples in Biometrika in 1933 — not to play slot machines, but to decide how to allocate patients between two candidate treatments as evidence about their effectiveness accumulated mid-trial.

The field didn't even have a name for the general problem until Herbert Robbins formalized “the bandit problem” in a 1952 paper, nearly two decades later. Clinical trials are still one of Thompson sampling's biggest real-world deployments today — the exact use case it was invented for, unchanged for over ninety years.

What a table of formulas can't give you is the feel of watching four strategies run the same hundred pulls and visibly diverge — one flailing at a fixed ε, one converging fast, one occasionally getting unlucky with its own optimism. That's worth seeing live, on real hidden payouts, not just read about.

Four arms was generous. Real casinos — and the toy version of the problem — have exactly one state, which is what made it tractable enough to solve in 1933. Formalize or drown. Chapter 1 waved at “agent,” “state,” and “reward” as if everyone already agreed what they meant. Chapter 2 shrank the whole problem down to one room with four levers so the explore/exploit tension would show up with nothing else in the frame. Neither one is a machine you can build. To build something, you need five symbols, in exactly this order, showing up in essentially every reinforcement learning paper published since the 1950s: (S, A, P, R, γ). That tuple is a Markov Decision Process, and it's the entire mathematical universe the rest of this article lives inside.

Five Symbols, One World

S is the set of states — every situation the agent could ever be in. A is the set of actions available from a given state. Put a concrete world under those two letters and they stop being abstract. Here's the kind of world this article keeps coming back to — this one 5×5, 25 squares, walls the agent can't walk through, one trap, one goal (later chapters grow the map; the grammar stays the same):

Twenty-five states. Four actions from most of them (up, down, left, right — fewer wherever a wall blocks the way). An agent starting in the bottom-left corner has to reach the top-right corner without wandering into the square marked −100, ideally in as few steps as possible. That's the whole game. It looks trivial because it is trivial, on purpose. Later chapters in this article all train something inside this exact map: value iteration floods it with numbers in Act II, a live Q-learner solves it on-screen at that act's climax, and Act IV rebuilds it as a cleaning robot whose reward function you get to sabotage. Learn to read this grid now and every one of those chapters is free.

P is the transition function — P(s′|s,a), the probability of landing in state s′ given that you were in state s and took action a. In a deterministic world, P collapses to a certainty: move right, you go right, every time. Almost nothing worth automating is that polite. Robot wheels slip. The gridworld example most textbooks reach for — Russell & Norvig's classic 4×3 maze — makes every action succeed only 80% of the time, with a 10% chance of sliding 90° left of where you meant to go and a 10% chance of sliding 90° right. P is the part of the formalism that admits the floor might be wet.

R is the reward function, a scalar attached to a transition. In the gridworld above: +100 for reaching the goal, −100 for stepping on the trap, and a small −1 for every ordinary step — not because moving costs anything physical, but because a flat step penalty is how you tell the agent that a ten-step path beats a thirty-step path to the same reward. Take the step cost away and a policy that wanders forever before eventually finding the goal scores exactly as well as one that walks straight there. R is where you encode not just what you want, but how much you're willing to pay in time to get it — a decision that turns out to be far more dangerous than it sounds. A later act spends an entire run of chapters on what goes wrong when R says something other than what you meant.

γ Is an Interest Rate

The last symbol, γ (gamma), is a number between 0 and 1 that answers a question none of the other four symbols touch: how much do you care about a reward that arrives later rather than now? Think of it as an interest rate running in reverse. A reward tomorrow is worth γ of a reward today; a reward two steps away is worth γ² of it; ten steps away, γ10. Small γ makes the agent myopic — it'll grab a small reward now over a huge one a few steps later. γ close to 1 makes the agent patient enough to walk past the trap for the sake of the goal three rows further on.

γ also sets an effective planning horizon: roughly 1/(1−γ) steps ahead actually move the numbers. At γ=0.99, that's about 100 steps of real lookahead — which is why 0.99 shows up constantly in RL papers doing anything longer than a toy problem. Push γ to 0.999 and the horizon stretches to a thousand steps; drop it to 0.9 and the agent barely plans past the next ten.

A Screenshot Beats a History

The Markov property is the assumption quietly underneath all five symbols: the current state has to contain everything relevant to the future. Not the path you took to get there — just where you are now. A chess board satisfies this; the legal moves and their consequences depend only on the current position, never on the sequence of moves that produced it. A single screenshot outperforms the entire game history, because the history adds nothing the screenshot didn't already contain. Break this property — hide information the state doesn't capture — and every algorithm in this article gets measurably worse, quietly, without telling you why.

The Thing You're Actually Shopping For

Four of the five symbols — S, A, P, R — describe the world. They're given, or at least discoverable; nobody trains them (P and R can be estimated, but they're properties of the environment, not choices you get to make). The fifth thing, the one that's actually the product of everything in this article, isn't in the tuple at all: it's the policy, π, a mapping from states to actions — or, more generally, from states to a probability distribution over actions. Every algorithm from here on is a different method for improving π. Value iteration computes it indirectly by first scoring every state. Q-learning computes it by scoring every state-action pair. Policy gradient methods skip the scoring and adjust π directly. They disagree completely on method and agree completely on target: a function that, given where you are, tells you what to do.

The map is drawn. Every square in that gridworld has a coordinate, a set of legal moves, and a reward waiting at two of its cells. What it doesn't have yet is a value — a number, for every square, saying how good it is to be standing there, accounting for every reward reachable from it and how far away each one is. That number doesn't exist anywhere in the tuple you just learned. Computing it is the entire next act, and it starts with an equation short enough to fit on one line and stubborn enough to have anchored forty years of the field.

LEARNING FROM A TABLE
How do values flow backward from the future?

CH 04 · ACT IIThe Bellman Equation

One line of recursion the whole field orbits: what a square is worth equals what you grab right now, plus a discounted rumor of everything after.
Value floods backward across a map, one sweep at a time — watch it happen below._

Move the agent one square from the goal and it has no idea it's close. Not until value has flowed there from somewhere else. A map tells you where the walls are, where the trap sits, where the reward waits — but a map with no prices on its squares is just a maze with better lighting. Pricing every square is this chapter's entire job, and it comes down to one recursive sentence: the value of where you stand is the reward you grab right now, plus γ times the value of where you land next.

Formally, that sentence has a name: the value function. V(s) is the expected total discounted reward an agent collects starting from state s and following some policy π forever after. Q(s,a) is the same idea one notch more specific — the expected return from state s, taking action a right now, then following π. V prices a place. Q prices a move. That distinction looks cosmetic here; you'll spend chapter 6 living inside it.

Both obey the same recursion, because the future doesn't care how you got there — the Markov property from chapter 3, doing its job again. The Bellman equation writes it out: the value of a state is the immediate reward plus the discounted value of whatever comes next, averaged over wherever your action might actually land you. Push it to its logical extreme — assume the agent always picks the best available action, forever — and you get the Bellman optimality equation: the definition of V* and Q*, the value functions of the best policy that could possibly exist.

Think of value as a rumor. The goal square starts out the only place with real news: “+100 lives here.” Every square touching it hears the rumor first-hand and marks itself up — a little less than +100, discounted by γ for the walk over. The squares touching those squares hear a second-hand version, worth a little less again. Run this long enough and the rumor reaches every reachable square, fainter with distance, and settles into a stable price. That settling process is value iteration — and the demo below lets you start the rumor yourself.

Bellman optimality, for the state-value function and its action-indexed twin:

V*(s)   = max_a  Σ_s' P(s'|s,a) [ R(s,a,s') + γ·V*(s') ]

Q*(s,a) = Σ_s' P(s'|s,a) [ R(s,a,s') + γ·max_a' Q*(s',a') ]

π*(s) = argmax_a Q*(s,a)

Value iteration turns the first line into an update rule — Vk+1(s) ← max_a Σ_s' P(s'|s,a)[R + γ·Vk(s')] — and repeats it until V stops moving. It provably converges: the Bellman backup is a γ-contraction, meaning each sweep shrinks the largest error anywhere on the map by at least a factor of γ, guaranteed, no matter where you started. Banach's fixed-point theorem doing quiet work behind the scenes.

A Name Nobody Could Object To

Bellman didn't call this recursion “dynamic programming” because it involves programming, or because anything about it is especially dynamic. He developed it at RAND in the early 1950s, under Department of Defense funding, and the Secretary of Defense at the time had a well-documented aversion to the word “research.” Bellman needed a name for his multistage decision theory that no Congressman could possibly object to — something that sounded urgent (“dynamic”) and familiar (“programming,” in the military sense of a schedule or plan, not software). By his own account, it was “something not even a Congressman could object to.” The equation survived on its merits. The name survived as cover.

Value iteration isn't the only route to V*. Its sibling, policy iteration, alternates two different steps instead of sweeping one equation over and over: freeze a policy and solve for its exact value everywhere (policy evaluation), then make the policy greedy with respect to that value (policy improvement), then repeat. Each round trip costs more than a single value-iteration sweep, but policy iteration usually needs far fewer round trips to finish. Both climb toward the same fixed point, V* and π*, from different directions — and that fixed point is what the rest of this chapter makes concrete with actual numbers.

The Cost of Patience

Contraction sounds cheap until you price it. To shrink the worst-case error 1000×, you need about log(1000)/log(1/γ) sweeps. At γ = 0.9, that's roughly 66. Push γ to 0.99 — a policy that plans 100 steps ahead instead of 10 — and it jumps to roughly 687. A 10× longer memory costs a 10× longer wait, and every single sweep touches every state. On a 5×5 grid, that's free. On anything bigger, it stops being free fast.

And “every sweep touches every state” hides a worse assumption underneath it: to run any of this, you need P(s'|s,a) — the actual physics of the world, the full table of what happens when you take an action. Real environments don't hand you that table. You don't know the odds a chess move leads to checkmate. You don't know the transition probabilities of a robot arm's motor, or a customer's next click. Value iteration is correct and it is useless, because it assumes an oracle you will never have. What you do have is experience — the agent can act, and watch what happens next. The next chapter throws out P entirely and learns the same value function from nothing but that.

CH 05 · ACT IILearning From a Guess

You don't wait for a road trip to end before updating your ETA. Every sign you pass is new evidence, folded in immediately.
This chapter's number is δ — three keystrokes that also happen to be running inside your skull right now._

You don't wait for the trip to end to update your ETA. You pass a sign, hit traffic, take a detour — and revise, on the spot, using a guess about the rest of the drive you haven't taken yet. Value iteration, from the last chapter, can't do that. It needs the whole map, P, before it sweeps a single cell. Most agents never get a map. They get one transition — a state, an action, a reward, whatever square they land on next — and have to learn from exactly that, with no dress rehearsal and no P.

This is temporal-difference learning, TD(0), and it may be the single most important trick in the field. Instead of waiting for the true return — the sum of every discounted reward from now until the episode ends — it updates V(s) toward a target built from one real reward and an estimate of everything after:

V(s) ← V(s) + α [ r + γV(s') - V(s) ]

Read the bracket as a single quantity, because it has a name that will follow you for the rest of this article — the TD error:

δ = r + γV(s′) − V(s)

δ is the gap between what you just guessed and what you now believe, one step later. r + γV(s') is the new evidence — a real reward plus a discounted estimate of the future. V(s) is the old guess. If they match, δ = 0 and nothing changes: your prediction was correct, no update needed. If reality beat the guess, δ is positive — nudge the estimate up. If reality fell short, δ is negative — nudge it down. α controls how hard you nudge. That's the whole algorithm. No model of the world, no waiting for the credits to roll. You bootstrap a guess from a newer guess, and the newer guess is only one real number away from the truth.

Monte Carlo is honest but slow to trust: with a hundred moves still left in the episode, its target is noisy. TD is fast but starts out lying to itself — V is wrong on step one, so the very first targets are wrong too. Wrong on purpose, though, in a self-correcting way: every time δ gets folded back in, V moves a little closer to being consistent with itself, and the error shrinks. That self-correction is also, biologically, not a metaphor.

The Brain Runs This

In 1997, Wolfram Schultz recorded dopamine neurons in a monkey's midbrain while it learned that a light predicted a squirt of juice a moment later. What he found, published with Peter Dayan and Read Montague in Science, is the reason this article's spine metaphor gets to skip the word “like.”

Before the monkey had learned anything, the juice itself made the neurons fire — a sharp burst, right on schedule. Unsurprising; reward should feel like something. What happened next is the finding that mattered. Once the monkey learned that the light reliably preceded the juice, the burst moved. It stopped firing at the juice and started firing at the light instead — the earliest reliable predictor of reward, not the reward itself. By the time learning had converged, delivering the juice exactly on schedule produced no response at all. The neurons had come to expect it, and expectation is not news.

Then Schultz withheld the juice on some trials, after the light had already fired the neurons up. The response didn't just fail to appear — it dropped below its resting baseline, at exactly the moment the juice was due. Fire on surprise. Go silent on confirmation. Dip on disappointment. That is not a description of pleasure. It is a description of an error signal — the gap between a prediction and what actually happened, computed and broadcast the instant new evidence arrives.

Run that pattern back through the equation. δ = r + γV(s′) − V(s). Before learning, V(s) is near zero everywhere, so the reward itself produces a large positive δ — the burst. Withhold the reward, and V(s) was expecting a payoff that r didn't deliver — δ goes negative. Three trial types, one formula, no exceptions.

TD-Gammon, 1992

Gerald Tesauro, at IBM's Thomas J. Watson Research Center, trained a neural network to play backgammon using nothing but TD(λ) and self-play — no opening book, no human game records, no hand-built evaluation function. It also changed the game itself. TD-Gammon's opening moves broke with decades of accepted theory, and some of the breaks stuck: slotting the five-point on an opening roll of 4-1 or 5-1 — textbook doctrine for generations — is no longer considered best play, because a machine tried something else two million times and it worked better.

δ told you how good arriving somewhere was. It never told you which move gets you there — for that you'd still need P, the transition model this chapter just deleted. The fix is to stop scoring states and start scoring state–action pairs directly: Q(s,a) instead of V(s). Do that, and something stranger than TD(0) falls out almost for free — an agent that can act like an idiot, stumbling and exploring and making ε-greedy mistakes on purpose, and still learn the value of the perfect policy it isn't even following. You'll get to do that on purpose in a few paragraphs. First, the machinery that makes it possible to do at all.

Chapter 5 left you with V(s) and no way to act on it: knowing a square is worth 8.1 doesn't tell you which of four neighboring moves gets you closer to it, not without P. Q(s,a) fixes that by scoring the move directly, not just the destination. And once you're scoring moves, TD(0)'s bootstrap trick gets a genuinely strange upgrade — Watkins' Q-learning update:

Q(s,a) ← Q(s,a) + α [ r + γ·max_a' Q(s',a') - Q(s,a) ]

Same skeleton as TD(0). Same δ, recomputed one layer down. One new piece: max_a' Q(s',a'), sitting exactly where “whatever I actually do next” used to be.

Read the update rule left to right as a sentence: “My new belief about this move equals my old belief, nudged by α toward the reward I just got plus the value of the best move I could imagine making next, minus what I believed a second ago.” The nudge is δ — the exact same surprise signal from chapter 5, just computed on actions instead of states. The strange part is max. It doesn't ask what you'll actually do next. It asks what the best possible version of you would do next, even if the real you, one ε-roll later, is about to do something else entirely. That single word is why Q-learning can afford to be reckless while it learns.

Convergence isn't automatic. Watkins, 1989

Chris Watkins introduced Q-learning in his 1989 Cambridge PhD thesis, Learning from Delayed Rewards, along with an informal argument for why it should work. The rigorous proof — that Q-learning converges to Q* with probability 1 under the conditions above — didn't arrive until Watkins and Peter Dayan published it three years later, in 1992. The algorithm worked before anyone could fully prove why.

That max has a second consequence nobody asked for: it makes Q-learning off-policy. The policy generating its data — ε-greedy, occasionally random — is not the policy its updates describe, which is the pure greedy one. Q-learning learns about a policy it never actually runs. SARSA, the alternative, refuses that trick: it plugs in the action it's actually about to take next, sampled from the same ε-greedy policy doing the acting.

Q-learning — off-policy

Q(s,a) ← Q(s,a) + α[r + γ·max_a' Q(s',a') − Q(s,a)]

Learns the value of the greedy policy, regardless of what it actually just did. It can act clumsy and still learn to act perfectly.

SARSA — on-policy

Q(s,a) ← Q(s,a) + α[r + γ·Q(s',a') − Q(s,a)]

a' is the action it actually takes next, ε-slip included. It learns the value of the policy it's really running — mistakes and all.

Learning about a policy you don't run is what makes Q-learning powerful — it can learn optimal behavior from a clumsy, exploring, even random data-gathering process. It's also what makes it dangerous to scale: chapter 7's Deadly Triad is off-policy learning's bill coming due once a neural network, not a table, is doing the estimating. File that away. For now, on a grid this small, off-policy just means the agent can afford to be reckless while it learns.

The fix has a name every supervised-learning engineer already knows: generalization. Replace the table with a parametric function — a neural network, Q(s,a;θ) — with far fewer weights θ than there are states. Now a state you have never seen gets a Q-value anyway, borrowed from the weights that similar states already shaped. You never visit 2.1×10170 Go positions; you visit a few million during self-play, and the network's weights carry what it learned to every position that even vaguely resembles them.

That borrowing is also the danger. A tabular update to row s touches exactly row s. A gradient update to θ touches every state whose features overlap with s — including, sometimes, the very state your bootstrapped target depends on. Fix one estimate and you can accidentally move the target you were trying to fix it against. Chase that target and it moves again. In Atari terms, two chapters from now: a network taught to value "ball near paddle, three bricks left" cannot help but also nudge its opinion of "ball near paddle, four bricks left" — the two states share nearly every pixel, and the network has no lever to treat them independently even if it wanted to. Reinforcement learning has an exact name for the failure mode this creates.

The Deadly Triad

Sutton and Barto call it the deadly triad: bootstrapping, off-policy learning, and function approximation. Combine all three and there is no proof your value estimates converge — and a documented history of them shooting off to infinity instead.

Baird's Star

The classic version of this trap is Leemon Baird's counterexample (1995, Residual Algorithms) — a seven-state “star” MDP with hand-picked linear features, built specifically to make off-policy TD with linear function approximation diverge no matter how small the learning rate. It's still the example every RL course reaches for when it needs to prove instability isn't a bug in someone's code — it's a property of the math.

The flip side matters just as much: Tsitsiklis & Van Roy (1997) proved that on-policy TD with linear function approximation does converge, to a well-defined (if not always optimal) fixed point. The triad isn't “neural nets are unstable.” It's a specific, narrow combination — and DQN, two chapters from now, sits exactly inside it.

Notice what the triad is not: it is not a claim that deep reinforcement learning cannot work. It is a claim that nobody has proven it must. That gap between mathematical guarantee and empirical reality sat unclosed for two decades. TD-Gammon (ch. 5) dodged it — backgammon's dice force a naturally diverse, self-decorrelating stream of states, which accidentally launders away much of the off-policy correlation problem. Try the same recipe on a domain that doesn't hand you that luck for free — most of them don't — and 1990s-era neural TD would reliably oscillate, or diverge outright, or quietly forget everything it had learned an hour ago.

Not a Theorem

There is still no proof that deep Q-learning converges in general. Every deep RL paper you will read from here forward is reporting an empirical result, not a corollary. It works far more often than the raw math promises — but "far more often" is doing real work in that sentence.

So the field spent most of the 2000s treating "neural network + TD + off-policy" as something you avoided rather than something you fixed. What changed in 2013 wasn't a new convergence proof for the triad — nobody has one yet. What changed was two pieces of engineering, unglamorous enough that neither made it into a headline, that made the triad behave well enough to train on raw pixels, at scale, without exploding. Neither of them is a theorem. Both of them worked.

The Two Hacks

Neither of these is a new theorem about the deadly triad. Both are engineering answers to exactly the two legs that make it dangerous.

1. Experience replay attacks the correlation. Consecutive Atari frames are nearly identical — the ball moved four pixels, nothing else changed. Train online, in order, on that stream and you're doing gradient descent on a wildly non-i.i.d. sequence: the network overfits to whatever the current trajectory happens to be doing and can forget what it learned an hour ago the moment the trajectory changes. DQN instead stores every transition — (s, a, r, s′) — in a ring buffer of the 1,000,000 most recent frames, and trains on minibatches of 32 sampled uniformly at random from that buffer. Old and new experience mix. Each transition gets reused across many updates instead of touched once and discarded. The effective training distribution stops whipsawing with the live policy.

2. The target network attacks the bootstrap. Q-learning's update pulls Q(s,a) toward r + γ·maxa′Q(s′,a′;θ) — but if the same θ you're updating also computes that target, every gradient step moves the finish line. DQN freezes a second copy of the network, θ−, used only to compute targets, and copies the live weights into it every C = 10,000 parameter updates. For 10,000 steps at a stretch, the target holds still. You're no longer chasing a target that runs the instant you get close.

Neither Hack Is a Proof

The deadly triad's convergence guarantee doesn't reappear because you added a replay buffer. What reappears is stability in practice — on most games, with one shared set of hyperparameters, most of the time. That "most" is the entire difference between a research curiosity and a system you can ship.

The Numbers

Each DQN agent trained for 50 million frames per game — about 38 days of continuous game experience, per the paper's own accounting, compressed into a training run that took a fraction of that in wall-clock time. ε annealed linearly from 1.0 to 0.1 over the first million frames, then held fixed at 0.1 for the rest of training — explore hard at the start, keep a 10% exploration floor forever after. Optimization used RMSProp over minibatches of 32, γ=0.99, frame-skip k=4 (the agent picks an action every 4th emulator frame and repeats it, which is also where "38 days" comes from — 50 million decisions, four emulator frames each).

One more hack, smaller but load-bearing: rewards were clipped to [−1, +1]. Without it, one shared learning rate can't work across a game that hands out 10-point increments and a game that hands out 1,000-point increments — the same hyperparameters would blow up on one and crawl on the other. Clipping is why 49 wildly different games could share one config. The cost is real, too: a clipped agent can't tell a 1-point win from a 100-point win. It only knows "positive."

Think of the replay buffer and target network as two separate clocks running at different speeds inside the same agent. The learning clock ticks every minibatch — fast, noisy, constantly adjusting. The target clock ticks once every 10,000 steps — slow, stable, a fixed reference the fast clock is allowed to chase without the reference itself flinching. Decorrelate the data, freeze the target: two independent fixes for two independent legs of the same triad.

The Breakout Tunnel

Deep into training, the Breakout agent stopped clearing bricks left-to-right the way a person does. It started digging — repeatedly driving the ball at one narrow column on the side of the brick wall until it punched a tunnel through, then letting the ball bounce in the gap between the ceiling and the remaining bricks, clearing dozens of them per life without the paddle touching anything. Nobody coded "dig a tunnel." Reward was pixels and score, nothing else. The strategy is visible in DQN's published gameplay footage, and it's the reason Breakout is one of the games where DQN's normalized score (Figure 3 of the Nature paper) blows past 1,000% of the human baseline — 1,327%, specifically. The network found an exploit a casual human player mostly doesn't.

What DQN still can't do is choose an action that isn't on a discrete menu. Its output layer has one unit per button combination — fine for 4 or 18 discrete actions, meaningless for a joint torque, a steering angle, anything continuous. Max over 18 numbers is a lookup. Max over every real number between −1 and 1 is an optimization problem you'd have to solve at every single timestep, for every action, forever. What if you stopped scoring actions altogether, and started improving the thing that picks them, directly?

Everything since chapter 4 has learned a value — V(s) or Q(s,a) — and derived a policy from it as an afterthought: whichever action has the highest Q, take that one. Chapter 8 just showed you where that afterthought breaks: argmax over a continuous action space isn't a lookup, it's an optimization problem you'd have to re-solve at every timestep. And a deterministic "always take the argmax" policy is sometimes provably the wrong kind of policy to have — in a game with a genuine mixed-strategy optimum (rock-paper-scissors is the toy case; poker is the real one), the best policy is a specific probability distribution, not a single best move, and no value function that outputs one number per action can express that on its own.

So skip the value function as the primary object. Parameterize the policy directly: π(a|s;θ) — a probability distribution over actions, output by a network with weights θ. Discrete actions get a softmax over logits. Continuous actions get something like a Gaussian, mean μ(s;θ) and spread σ(s;θ), and you literally sample the action from it. Exploration is no longer a bolted-on ε-greedy dice roll layered on top of a greedy policy — it's built into the distribution's own width. A wide σ explores. A narrow σ commits.

The Policy Gradient Theorem

The question is how to adjust θ when all you get back is a scalar return, arriving at the end of an episode, with no gradient attached to it. The policy gradient theorem answers it, and in plain speech it says exactly one thing:

∇J(θ) = E[∇log π(a|s;θ) · R]

Increase the log-probability of the action you took, in proportion to how well it went. Do that for every action in every episode, averaged over enough episodes, and probability mass drifts toward whatever the agent actually did when things went well — and away from whatever it did when things went badly.

∇θ log π(a|s;θ) is a direction in parameter space: which way to move θ to make the action you just took more likely next time. That part is identical to ordinary classification — it's the same log-derivative direction cross-entropy loss uses. The difference is what multiplies it. Cross-entropy multiplies by a fixed weight of 1, because the label is always "correct." Here, R is a number that can be large and positive, mildly positive, zero, or sharply negative — and its sign literally flips the direction of the update. Nobody told the agent which action was "correct." The return told it how that particular choice turned out, and the gradient pushes accordingly.

REINFORCE (Williams, 1992) is the direct implementation: run a full episode, compute its total return R(τ), and for every state-action pair the agent actually visited, nudge θ by α·∇log π(a|s;θ)·R(τ). It's unbiased — averaged over infinite episodes, this gradient estimate genuinely points toward better θ. It's also, on its own, close to useless for anything but toy problems.

The Variance Problem

A single trajectory is a terrible measurement of a single action's quality. A genuinely good decision, five steps into a long episode, can still land inside a low-return trajectory because of bad luck twelve steps later — and REINFORCE punishes it anyway, because the entire trajectory's return gets attached to every action in it, indiscriminately. A mediocre decision inside a lucky trajectory gets rewarded the same way. The gradient estimate from one episode is enormous variance wrapped around a real but faint signal.

Averaging kills variance slowly. Standard error of a Monte Carlo estimate shrinks as 1/√N — to cut the noise in your gradient estimate in half, you need four times the trajectories, not two. High-variance gradients mean either tiny, overly cautious step sizes, or large steps that occasionally throw the policy somewhere it shouldn't be. Either way: slow.

The Baseline Fix

The fix doesn't reduce the number of episodes you need — it reduces the noise in each one. Subtract any state-dependent baseline b(s) from the return before multiplying it into the gradient: ∇log π(a|s;θ)·(R − b(s)). This introduces zero bias, for a reason worth sitting with: averaged over actions sampled from π, E[∇log π(a|s;θ)] is exactly zero at every fixed state — so subtracting anything that doesn't depend on which action you took can't shift the expected gradient. It can only cancel noise that both good and bad trajectories through the same state share.

The natural choice of baseline is b(s) = V(s) — the value function you already know how to learn, from chapters 4 through 6. Subtracting it turns the raw return into something with a name: the advantage, A(s,a) = R − V(s).

Better Than Expected, Not Good in Absolute Terms

An advantage of +3 doesn't mean "this action earned +3." It means "this action beat what you'd normally expect from this state by 3." An action inside a genuinely unlucky episode can still carry a positive advantage — it outperformed the state's own baseline, even if the final number wasn't impressive. An action inside a lucky episode can carry a negative advantage — the weak link the luck was covering for. Advantage isolates credit to the decision, not the trajectory it happened to be riding in.

That subtraction — compare the outcome to your own expectation, not to zero — is the same arithmetic move as the TD error back in chapter 5: δ = r + γV(s′) − V(s). Different lineage, same idea, same dopamine signal. Value-based methods and policy-gradient methods look like two different fields until you notice they both converged on "measure surprise relative to your own prediction" as the unit of learning.

In practice nobody waits for the variance to average itself out over millions of solo episodes. A3C (Mnih et al., 2016) runs REINFORCE-with-a-baseline across 16 parallel actors on ordinary CPU cores — no GPU — each collecting decorrelated experience simultaneously, bootstrapping short n-step returns instead of waiting for a whole episode to end. It matched or beat GPU-trained DQN variants across 57 games in roughly half the training time. Parallelism and a baseline did what more patience alone couldn't.

The Acronym Is Real

REINFORCE isn't just a name Williams liked — it's a backronym for the update rule itself: REward Increment = Nonnegative Factor × Offset Reinforcement × Characteristic Eligibility. Strip the jargon and it's the same three pieces this chapter just built: a learning rate, a baseline-offset reward, and the gradient term telling you which weights caused the action. 1992, and the algorithm is still the starting point every modern policy-gradient method is a variation on.

You now have an actor that acts, and — the moment you subtract a baseline — something that looks a great deal like a critic grading it. Formalize that split and one failure mode remains completely uncured. In supervised learning, a bad gradient step costs you one epoch; the training set doesn't change underneath you. In reinforcement learning, the policy is the thing generating your next batch of data. Push θ too far in one update and every trajectory you collect afterward comes from the broken policy you just created. You need a leash.

A bad step costs supervised learning one epoch. In RL, it can cost everything.

Here is why chapter 9's closing warning is not hypothetical. Your training data in reinforcement learning is not a fixed CSV sitting on disk — it is trajectories sampled from the current policy, generated fresh every batch. Push θ too far in one gradient step and π can land somewhere that produces nonsense actions. The next batch of trajectories, sampled from that broken policy, is now garbage. You train on the garbage. The policy gets worse, not better, and there is no "next epoch of clean data" to bail you out — the data is the policy, and the policy is broken. This is not a bad epoch. It's catastrophic, and often irreversible without restarting from an earlier checkpoint. A policy that just learned to stand up can, from one oversized step, collapse into one that faceplants on every rollout — and every rollout after that is sampled from the faceplanter, so there is no clean gradient left anywhere in the pipeline to climb back out with.

Trust Regions

TRPO (Schulman et al., 2015) was the first serious fix: constrain every update so the KL divergence between the new policy and the old one stays under a small threshold, and solve that constrained problem with second-order machinery — a natural-gradient direction approximated via conjugate gradient, followed by a backtracking line search that shrinks the step until both the KL constraint and a guaranteed-improvement bound hold. It works, and it comes with an actual monotonic-improvement proof under the derived surrogate objective — a real theorem, unlike the empirical truce DQN made with the deadly triad. The cost is the machinery itself: Fisher-vector products, conjugate gradient, a whole second-order code path bolted onto what is otherwise ordinary policy gradient. Nobody wanted to reimplement that for every new project.

Take the Pessimistic Estimate

min() as "assume the worse of two plausible outcomes" is a pattern that recurs across safe RL and beyond — every time you clip, cap, or discount an estimate specifically to avoid rewarding an update for optimism you can't yet verify, you're doing PPO's trick under a different name.

PPO is not a research curiosity. It is the default optimizer behind OpenAI Five, the Dota 2 system that trained across 256 GPUs and 128,000 CPU cores, self-play generating roughly 180 years of Dota 2 experience every day — the same clipped-ratio update from the diagram above, unmodified in its core mechanics, running at a scale where a bad step would be catastrophically expensive to recover from. 180 years of self-play a day is more Dota than any single human could play across several lifetimes — and PPO's clip is the only thing standing between that firehose of experience and a policy update large enough to erase months of training in one pass. It's also, a few chapters ahead, the algorithm doing the actual weight updates inside RLHF at every lab running it.

Picture θ on a leash held by θold. Every update, the leash resets to its full length — r(θ)=1 — and the dog is free to run in whichever direction the advantage points, right up until it hits the end of the leash at r=1±ε. TRPO measures the leash's length precisely, every single step, with an expensive second-order ruler. PPO just ties a knot at 1±ε and lets ordinary gradient descent do the walking.

Twelve Pages

Proximal Policy Optimization Algorithms (Schulman et al., 2017) is twelve pages long, and roughly half of them are figures. It doesn't out-argue TRPO theoretically — it doesn't even try to. It won adoption because it is a five-line diff from a vanilla policy-gradient implementation you probably already had, with none of TRPO's second-order plumbing. Simplicity, not elegance, is why it became the default RL algorithm for the rest of the decade.

You now have machinery that can take any reward function you can write down and reliably, safely, at any scale you can afford GPUs for, push a policy toward maximizing it. Not approximately — reliably. That sentence should terrify you a little, and the next act is about exactly why.

Every number you can write down for a reward is a proxy. Push an optimizer hard enough against it and the proxy wins, the goal loses._

The boat never finished a single race. It won anyway. In 2016, OpenAI researchers Dario Amodei and Jack Clark turned an RL agent loose in CoastRunners, a boat-racing game that pays points for hitting checkpoint targets along the course, not for crossing the finish line first. The agent found an isolated lagoon holding three turbo-boost targets, learned to loop through all three just as they respawned, and stayed there — catching fire, crashing into walls and other boats, going the wrong way down the track, never once finishing — while its score climbed to roughly 20% higher than any human had ever posted. The designers wrote “collect points.” They meant “win the race.” The agent read the function, not the mind of the person who wrote it.

This has a name older than reinforcement learning. In 1975 the economist Charles Goodhart observed that any statistical regularity used as a policy target tends to collapse the moment you lean on it. Two decades later the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern compressed that into the sentence everyone actually quotes: “When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.” A reward function is a measure. An RL agent is the most literal optimizer ever built. Hand it a proxy for the goal, apply enough optimization pressure, and the proxy detaches from the thing it was standing in for — the boat learns to farm turbo pickups, not to race.

The popular illustration of this is the cobra effect: colonial Delhi supposedly paid a bounty on dead cobras, enterprising residents started breeding cobras to collect it, the government cancelled the program, and the now-worthless snakes got released into the streets — leaving the city with more cobras than it started with. It’s a vivid story, and it’s probably not true. The term was coined by the economist Horst Siebert in a 2001 book, built on an anecdote with no contemporary primary source, and a 2025 investigation by the Friends of Snakes Society turned up no historical record backing it. Keep the shape of the failure, not the snake count: reward the metric (“cobras turned in”) instead of the goal (“fewer cobras”), and the two can point in opposite directions.

Optimization Pressure Is The Variable

None of the agents in this chapter cheated. Each one found the literal maximum of the function it was handed. The lesson isn’t “AI is deceptive” — it’s that a reward function is a hypothesis about what you want, and every hypothesis has edge cases. The more compute you spend searching for the maximum, the further into those edge cases the search reaches.

Reward hacking didn’t start with deep learning, and it isn’t hypothetical. DeepMind safety researcher Victoria Krakovna has kept a running public list of documented specification-gaming cases since 2018 — “Specification gaming: the flip side of AI ingenuity” — and every row cites a paper, a demo, or a bug report. Sparse rewards — win, lose, +100 at the goal — are hard to learn from: nothing tells the agent it’s getting warmer along the way. A model 100 times smaller just out-argued the one that trained it. In 2022, OpenAI’s InstructGPT paper reported that human labelers preferred outputs from a 1.3-billion-parameter model fine-tuned with human feedback over outputs from the full 175-billion-parameter GPT-3, despite the smaller model having more than 100× fewer parameters. Scale didn’t do that. Alignment did — and in this paper, alignment is not a value statement bolted on after the fact. It’s a training objective, with a loss function, computed by a second neural network that learned what “helpful” looks like from human comparisons.

The Three-Stage Pipeline

The pipeline has three stages, and each one exists only to feed the next.

Stage 1 — SFT. Take the pretrained GPT-3 base model and fine-tune it on human-written demonstrations: a prompt, and the response a labeler thinks a good assistant would give. InstructGPT used roughly 13,000 training prompts here. The result behaves reasonably — it just has no signal yet for “reasonably, compared to what.”

Stage 2 — the reward model. For a given prompt, sample several completions from the SFT model — labelers saw 4 to 9 per prompt — and have a human rank them best to worst. Every ranking decomposes into pairwise comparisons (best beats second, second beats third, and so on), producing roughly 33,000 prompts’ worth of comparisons in the training set. Those comparisons train a reward model: a network that reads a (prompt, response) pair and outputs one scalar score. OpenAI trained this reward model at only 6 billion parameters — smaller than every policy it would later grade, including the 175B one — because a 175B reward model turned out to be too unstable to double as the value function during RL.

Stage 3 — PPO against the reward model, with a leash back to the SFT policy so the policy can’t wander off and produce gibberish the RM happens to love. More on the leash below.

The Bradley-Terry model predates every neural network in this article by 66 years — Ralph Bradley and Milton Terry published it in Biometrika in 1952 to rank incomplete tournaments. The Leash, Again

PPO alone, chasing the reward model with no restraint, finds the RM’s blind spots fast — sentences stuffed with whatever keyword pattern the 6B network associates with quality, hedges and disclaimers repeated for their own sake, fluent text that scores well and says nothing. The fix is the leash from Chapter 10: penalize the policy, per token, for drifting from the distribution the SFT model would have produced.

The KL-leashed objective 
reward(x, y) = RM(x, y) - β × KL( π_θ(y|x) || π_SFT(y|x) )

β is a dial. Too low, and the policy walks straight into the reward model’s exploits — confident, fluent, reward-maxed, and useless. Too high, and the KL term dominates the objective; the policy barely moves from where SFT left it, and none of the preference signal gets through. OpenAI’s PPO-ptx variant adds one more term — a slice of the original pretraining loss, mixed directly into the PPO gradient — specifically to stop the KL leash from making the model forget how to do everything GPT-3 could already do.

The Reward Model Is Also A Proxy

Learning the reward function doesn’t exit Goodhart’s law — it just moves the law up one level, from “the number the environment gives you” to “the number a 6B network trained on 33K human judgments gives you.” OpenAI’s follow-up study, Scaling Laws for Reward Model Overoptimization (Gao, Schulman & Hilton, 2022), made the recursion explicit: train a “gold” RM to stand in for real human judgment, then optimize a policy against a smaller proxy RM trained on the gold model’s labels. Push far enough against the proxy and its score keeps rising — while the gold score, the thing you actually wanted, peaks and then falls. The RM was gameable the whole time. It just took enough compute to find where.

RLHF solves specification by outsourcing it: instead of an engineer trying to write down “helpful” as a formula, a human says “this one, not that one” tens of thousands of times, and a network generalizes from there. That works when the only available signal is a person’s opinion. But opinion isn’t the only kind of signal in the world. A math proof is either valid or it isn’t. Code either passes the test suite or it doesn’t. For an entire category of problem, you don’t need a reward model, a preference dataset, or a human in the loop at all — you need a checker. What happens when the reward is simply correct?

DeepSeek skipped the reward model entirely, and it worked better. In January 2025, DeepSeek released R1-Zero: reinforcement learning applied directly to a base model — DeepSeek-V3-Base, 671B parameters, 37B active per token — with no supervised fine-tuning step at all, and no learned reward model either. The paper is explicit about why: “We do not apply the outcome or process neural reward model in developing DeepSeek-R1-Zero, because we find that the neural reward model may suffer from reward hacking.” That is Chapter 12’s Goodhart recursion, read back to you by the team that decided to route around it. Instead of training a network to guess what “good reasoning” looks like, they used a checker: does the boxed answer match the known solution, does the code pass its unit tests, does the response wrap its reasoning in <think> tags. Two rewards — accuracy and format — both of them functions, not networks. Nothing to overfit to, because there’s nothing learned in the loop at all.

Something happened that nobody scripted. The paper’s training curves show the model’s average response length climbing over the course of training — from roughly a thousand tokens per answer toward nearly ten thousand, thousands of steps later — as a side effect of the accuracy reward alone. The model was never told to think longer. It discovered that spending more tokens re-deriving and checking its own steps raised the odds of landing on the answer the checker wanted, and every time that paid off, the policy gradient reinforced spending more tokens on it. Reasoning length wasn’t a design choice. It was a purchased behavior.

Partway through training, the researchers caught the model doing something specific enough to name. Mid-derivation, unprompted, it produced: “Wait, wait. Wait. That’s an aha moment I can flag here. Let’s reevaluate this step-by-step to identify if the correct sum can be…” The paper calls this the aha moment — the point where R1-Zero starts interrupting its own reasoning, flagging a likely mistake, and backtracking, without ever having been shown a labeled example of what backtracking looks like. Self-verification wasn’t trained in as a behavior. It was discovered as a strategy, because catching your own error before the final answer wins more often than not catching it.

Nobody Wrote The Backtracking

R1-Zero was never shown a single example of self-correction — “wait, let me reconsider” was not in any training label. It emerged because a rule-based reward doesn’t care how you got the right answer, only that you did, and re-checking your own work turned out to be a cheap way to get there more often.

The Optimizer: GRPO

The algorithm behind all of this answers a question Chapters 9 and 10 left open. PPO needs a value network — a second model, roughly the size of the policy, whose entire job is estimating how good a state is so the advantage calculation has a baseline to subtract. Training and holding that second model in memory is expensive, and for a language model, credit assignment across every generated token multiplies the cost. GRPO — Group Relative Policy Optimization, introduced a year earlier in DeepSeek’s math-reasoning paper, DeepSeekMath (Shao et al., 2024), and reused for R1 — answers: don’t train a critic. Build the baseline out of the data you already have.

For a prompt, sample a group of G outputs from the current policy — G=16 for DeepSeek-R1’s RL stage, G=64 in the original DeepSeekMath experiments. Score every output in the group with the reward function. Then normalize within the group: subtract the group’s own mean, divide by the group’s own spread. The group is the baseline. An output that scores better than its siblings gets a positive advantage and its tokens get reinforced; an output that scores worse gets pushed down — relative to the other attempts at the same problem, not against a separately-trained estimate of value. As DeepSeekMath’s authors put it, GRPO “foregoes the critic model, instead estimating the baseline from group scores, significantly reducing training resources.” For R1-Zero specifically, where the reward is a rule instead of a network, that drops the model count in memory from PPO-RLHF’s four — policy, value, reward, reference — down to two: the policy being trained, and a frozen reference copy for the KL term.

None of this stayed locked inside a 671B model. DeepSeek distilled 800,000 reasoning traces generated by R1 into six smaller dense checkpoints — 1.5B, 7B, 8B, 14B, 32B, and 70B, built on Qwen and Llama backbones — teaching them the shape of R1’s reasoning at a fraction of the serving cost. The full distillation story, and what it did to the market’s assumptions about frontier spending, is its own chapter.

Zoom all the way out and the shape of the last three acts repeats. A pretrained base model is a library of everything pretraining could cram into its weights — most of it never touched, most of it irrelevant to any one conversation. Every technique in this article, from a four-armed bandit to a 671B model checking its own arithmetic, was a different answer to the same question: given a number that arrives late, how do you decide which parts of that library get pulled onto the shelf? RL is the librarian. It’s time to see what happens when the librarian runs the whole building.

Thirteen chapters on one idea: value flows backward through time, one δ at a time. This last one steps back far enough to ask what all that machinery is actually for — and what it still can't do._

Cut the reinforcement-learning stage and the library still stands — it just has no librarian. Every frontier model you've talked to this year was pretrained on something close to the entire public internet, a process that teaches uncanny fluency and zero opinion about which of its own sentences are worth producing. Predicting what a random web page says next and being useful to the person actually in front of you are different objectives, and the gap between them is closed by one thing: reinforcement learning. Not a bigger model. Not a longer context window. The last stage, the smallest slice of total compute in the whole pipeline, and the only stage that touches goals instead of statistics.

Watch how labs actually ship models and the pattern repeats everywhere: self-supervised pretraining builds the capability; a comparatively tiny RL stage — RLHF, GRPO, whatever a given lab is branding it this quarter — decides which of the capabilities already sitting in the weights get surfaced, rewarded, and repeated. A base model can usually already write correct Python before anyone tunes it; RLHF doesn't teach it Python, it teaches the model to choose to write Python instead of continuing the prompt as if it were an unanswered Stack Overflow question. DeepSeek-R1-Zero, from the last chapter, is the starkest version of this split: identical base weights, zero supervised fine-tuning, just a correctness signal and a few thousand RL steps — and the model starts checking its own arithmetic mid-sentence. Nothing was added to what it knew. Something was added to what it wanted.

The Bitter Lesson

In March 2019, Richard Sutton — the same Sutton whose name sits on TD-learning and the policy-gradient theorem, the researcher this entire article has been quietly walking through — posted a short essay that reads like a post-mortem on his own field. Its argument survives compression: seventy years of AI research show that methods which scale with computation — search and learning — beat methods built from encoded human expertise, once enough compute exists to run them. Not usually. Consistently. Chess engines built on grandmaster heuristics lost to Deep Blue's brute-force search; Go engines trained on centuries of human joseki lost to a system that discarded human game records entirely and learned from self-play instead; speech and vision followed the same curve, rule systems giving way to statistics, statistics giving way to learned representations at scale. Sutton calls it the bitter lesson on purpose — researchers spend years encoding what they know, it helps for a while, and it is eventually overtaken by a dumber-looking method that simply had more computation to spend.

RL Doesn't Illustrate the Bitter Lesson — It Is the Bitter Lesson

Sutton names exactly two techniques that scale without ceiling as compute grows: search and learning. Every algorithm in this article threads both at once — a policy searches the world through trial and error (rollouts, ε-greedy exploration, the group sampling in GRPO) while a value or policy update learns from whatever the search turns up. Delete the search half and you get supervised learning. Delete the learning half and you get a database of Monte Carlo returns. Reinforcement learning is the one mainstream paradigm built from both halves simultaneously — which is the actual reason it, and not a bigger transformer, ended up owning the last mile.

Exploration is the sharper version of a problem this article has been solving since the casino in chapter 2: optimism in the face of uncertainty works beautifully across four slot machines, where trying an unexplored arm costs you one pull. It buckles across a state space where the first hint of reward sits thousands of correct actions away and every wrong turn looks exactly as uninformative as every other wrong turn. Reward specification is the same story wearing a more dangerous costume — it never got solved between chapter 11 and chapter 12, it got a promotion. "Don't let the reward function lie to you" is a tractable engineering problem. "Don't let the most capable optimizer humans have ever built lie to itself about what you meant" is most of what the word alignment now means.

One active repair for the first problem, in a single breath: instead of learning a policy from millions of real environment steps, learn P — the transition function from chapter 3 that value iteration always assumed you had for free — as a neural network, then train the policy entirely inside rollouts that model imagines. That's Dreamer: a compact latent world model predicts what happens next, and an actor-critic never touches the real environment during training, only its own forecast of it. DreamerV3 used exactly this trick to become the first agent to mine a diamond in Minecraft from raw pixels with no human demonstrations and no curriculum — a reward so sparse that most published RL systems never see it once. It doesn't fix exploration or reward specification. It just makes every real step of experience worth a thousand imagined ones.

Zoom out far enough and the whole arc of this article collapses into one sentence: for three decades the field's hard problem was how to maximize a number — how to push value backward through delay, how to keep a Q-table from diverging, how to stop one oversized policy step from destroying a million good ones. Chapters 1 through 10 are that problem, solved with steadily increasing sophistication, Watkins' proof and PPO's clip and GRPO's free lunch all answering variations of the same question. Somewhere around a boat circling a lagoon on fire in chapter 11, the field's hard problem quietly became a different question. Not how to maximize the number. Which number. Engagement or honesty. A reward model's opinion or the user's actual satisfaction three exchanges later. Verifiable correctness or genuine understanding. Every algorithm in this article will faithfully optimize whatever you hand it — that was always the whole promise of the machinery, and it kept it. The open question was never in chapters 1 through 10. It's sitting in whatever you write on the reward line.
