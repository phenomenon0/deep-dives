The Efficiency Papers — Part V

01 // The 26-Year Idea

In 1991, four researchers at the University of Toronto published a nine-page paper about splitting a neural network into specialist sub-networks. Robert Jacobs, Michael Jordan, Steven Nowlan, and Geoffrey Hinton called their system "Adaptive Mixtures of Local Experts." A gating network would look at each input and decide which specialist should handle it. They tested it on vowel discrimination. It worked. Then nothing happened.

Not for a year. Not for five. For twenty-six years, the idea sat dormant. The paper — nine pages in Neural Computation, volume 3, issue 1 — contained the entire conceptual DNA of what would become the most important scaling technique in modern AI. Three principles, already fully formed in 1991: divide the problem into sub-problems (divide-and-conquer). Let a learned gate decide which specialist handles which input (soft competition). Never activate all the parameters at once (conditional computation). The architecture was right. The world was not.

The infrastructure gap

The gap was never about the theory being wrong. MoE requires spreading experts across multiple devices and routing tokens between them. In 1991, there were no GPU clusters. No high-bandwidth interconnects. No frameworks for distributed training. The all-to-all communication pattern that MoE demands — every token potentially traveling to any device — accounts for over 40% of total training runtime even on modern hardware. In the 1990s, network bandwidth was orders of magnitude worse.

Then there was the training instability problem. Without careful load balancing, MoE collapses. The gating network develops a preference for one or two experts, sends them more data, they improve faster, the gate sends even more, and the other experts starve. A self-reinforcing death spiral. The solutions — auxiliary losses, capacity factors, noise injection — required decades of engineering iteration that hadn't happened yet.

And for most of that gap, nobody needed it. From roughly 2012 to 2017, simply making models bigger and training longer kept working. Dense scaling was easy. AlexNet (2012) proved GPUs could train neural networks. Researchers doubled layer counts, doubled hidden dimensions, added more training data, and watched performance climb. The recipe was simple: more compute, more parameters, better results. When the straightforward path works, nobody explores the clever alternatives. There was no economic pressure to be clever about parameter efficiency.

By 2016, the economics began to shift. Training costs climbed into the millions. GPU clusters grew from dozens to thousands of accelerators. Dense scaling was becoming expensive. A 10x larger model needed 10x more compute, which needed 10x more GPUs, which needed 10x more money. The linear relationship between capability and cost was hitting budgets. Labs began asking: is there a way to get 10x the knowledge without 10x the compute?

At Google, where TPU pods offered the distributed infrastructure that MoE had always needed, the conditions finally aligned for someone to revisit the 1991 idea. The answer to "10x knowledge without 10x compute" was already 25 years old. It just needed the right hardware to prove it.

The constraint

MoE didn't fail in 1991. It was stranded. The idea needed distributed compute, high-bandwidth interconnects, and load-balancing algorithms — none of which existed until Google's TPU pods arrived two decades later.

Shazeer wakes the giant

In 2017, Noam Shazeer — the same researcher who would later invent Multi-Query Attention (Part I of this series) — published a paper with a title that reads like a dare: "Outrageously Large Neural Networks: The Sparsely-Gated Mixture-of-Experts Layer." The key insight: you can have a 137-billion-parameter model that only activates a tiny fraction of its parameters per input. Shazeer added up to thousands of feed-forward sub-networks, gated by a trainable sparse router with noisy top-k selection. The result was over 1,000x capacity increase with minor computational overhead.

He applied it as MoE layers sandwiched between stacked LSTMs for language modeling and machine translation. The model was tested at scales that sound quaint now — 68 billion and 137 billion parameters — but were extraordinary in 2017. For context, GPT-2 (the model that launched a thousand blog posts about AI risk) would have 1.5 billion parameters two years later in 2019. Shazeer's MoE model was 90x larger than GPT-2. But because most of those parameters were dormant for any given input, the actual computational cost per token was comparable to much smaller dense models. This is the MoE promise in one sentence: the knowledge of a giant with the compute cost of a model 10x smaller.

The hardware had finally caught up. Google's internal infrastructure — TPU pods with high-bandwidth interconnects — provided exactly the distributed substrate that MoE had always needed. GPU clusters existed elsewhere too. Frameworks could handle distributed training. The 26-year wait was over, and the next seven years would produce more MoE innovation than the preceding three decades combined.

The 26-Year Timeline
Hover over a milestone to see details. The idea waited for the hardware.

Look at the timeline. The right side — infrastructure milestones — fills in before the left side can resume. TPU pods in 2015. NVLink in 2016. Only then does Shazeer's paper appear in 2017. The idea didn't evolve slowly. It was frozen, waiting for the physical world to catch up.

Shazeer's paper is worth pausing on because it introduces a trick that every subsequent MoE system inherits: noisy top-k gating. The router computes a score for each expert, then adds learned Gaussian noise before selecting the top-k. The noise serves two purposes. During early training, it forces exploration — tokens visit experts they wouldn't otherwise choose, preventing premature specialization. Later, as the noise scale shrinks, routing stabilizes. It is a remarkably simple idea: randomness as a regularizer for discrete decisions.

The noise also helps with a fundamental tension in MoE training: the routing function is discrete (top-k is a hard selection), but gradient-based optimization requires continuous, differentiable functions. The noise smooths the decision boundary, making it possible for the optimizer to gradually shift tokens between experts rather than making abrupt all-or-nothing switches. Without noise, a token that barely prefers Expert A over Expert B (score 0.501 vs 0.499) would always go to Expert A, starving Expert B of training signal for that token type. With noise, the token sometimes visits Expert B, allowing both experts to learn from it. The noise converts a hard discrete decision into a soft probabilistic one, which is exactly what gradient descent needs to do its job.

The paper also reveals a practical reality that shapes everything that follows. Shazeer trained on a cluster where each expert lives on a different device. Every routing decision is a physical decision: which machine does this token travel to? The compute is cheap — matrix multiplications are what GPUs are built for. The communication is expensive — moving tensors across a network is what GPUs are not built for. This tension — cheap compute, expensive communication — will define every MoE design for the next seven years.

Consider the numbers. An H800 GPU can perform about 990 TFLOPS of FP8 computation. The same GPU has about 400 GB/s of NVLink bandwidth for inter-GPU communication. A typical MoE expert FFN computation on a small batch might take microseconds. The all-to-all dispatch and combine that surrounds it — sending tokens to the right GPU, gathering results back — can take milliseconds. Three orders of magnitude slower. The compute is essentially free compared to the communication.

This ratio is the central engineering challenge of MoE. The architecture insight (conditional computation) is powerful. The systems challenge (communication overhead) is brutal. Every MoE paper since 2017 has been, in some sense, a paper about managing communication costs. GShard used top-2 routing (more communication). Switch Transformer used top-1 (less communication). DeepSeek used FP8 dispatch (smaller tensors) and DualPipe (hidden latency). The innovation trajectory is a war against the network.

02 // Conditional Computation

Here is the principle that makes everything else in this article possible, and it sounds like cheating: not every parameter needs to fire for every input. You can have a model with 671 billion parameters and only activate 37 billion of them per token. The other 634 billion do nothing. They consume no compute. They generate no gradients for that token. They sit in memory, dormant, waiting for a token that needs them.

A standard transformer runs every token through every weight in the feed-forward network. The FFN is the largest component of each transformer block — roughly two-thirds of the parameters. MoE replaces that dense FFN with N parallel expert FFNs plus a router. The router examines each token and sends it to its top-k experts. Only those experts activate. The rest sleep.

The result is a strange and powerful decoupling. Total parameters can be massive — that is the model's knowledge capacity, how much it can store. But per-token compute stays small — that is the model's active parameters, how much work it does per inference step. A dense 70B model activates 70 billion parameters for every token. A sparse 671B model might activate 37 billion. More knowledge, less work.

Think of it physically. A dense model is a library where you must read every book before answering any question. An MoE model is a library with a librarian at the door who knows which shelf has your answer. The library can be enormous — millions of books — but you only ever pull a handful off the shelf. The librarian (the router) is the key innovation. Everything depends on the quality of the routing decision.

The decoupling of knowledge capacity from active compute has a direct economic consequence. In a dense model, doubling quality (roughly) means doubling parameters, which doubles per-token compute, which doubles inference cost. In an MoE model, doubling quality means doubling experts (doubling knowledge capacity) while keeping top-k constant. Per-token compute stays the same. Inference speed stays the same. You only pay more for storage, not for computation. The cost of quality scales with storage, not with compute. This is the fundamental economic advantage of sparsity.

Dense Transformer

Every token hits every parameter. A 70B model does 70B FLOPs of work per token. Knowledge capacity = active compute.

Sparse MoE

Each token hits a fraction of parameters. A 671B model does 37B FLOPs per token. Knowledge capacity = 18x active compute.

Dense vs Sparse — One Transformer Layer 
DENSE (e.g., LLaMA 405B):
token → [Attention] → [FFN: all 405B params] → output
                          every token, every parameter

SPARSE MoE (e.g., DeepSeek-V3):
token → [Attention] → [Router] → [Expert 12]  ↵
                                  → [Expert 47]  ↵ [Combine] → output
                                  → [Expert 183] ↵
                                  → [...5 more]  ↵
                                  → [Shared Expert] (always on)
          8 of 256 routed + 1 shared = 37B of 671B

The router is the decision point. It computes a score for each expert — typically a dot product between the token's representation and each expert's learned centroid — and selects the top-k. In DeepSeek-V3, k=8. The selected experts process the token in parallel, their outputs are weighted by the router's scores, and the weighted sum becomes the layer's output. The 248 non-selected experts do nothing. Their weights are not loaded, their compute is not consumed. They simply do not participate.

MoE Forward Pass// For each token in the sequence:
scores = router(token_hidden_state)  // [256] affinity scores
top_k_indices = topk(scores, k=8)  // select 8 of 256
weights = softmax(scores[top_k_indices])

output = 0
for i in top_k_indices:
    output += weights[i] * expert_ffn[i](token_hidden_state)
output += shared_expert(token_hidden_state)  // always active

// Total compute: 8 small FFNs + 1 shared FFN
// 248 experts: zero compute, zero memory access

GShard: the scale proof

In 2020, a Google team led by Dmitry Lepikhin and HyoukJoong Lee scaled Transformer-based MoE to 600 billion parameters on 2,048 TPU v3 cores. They called it GShard. Training took under four days.

The efficiency numbers were staggering. Scaling from 37.5B to 600B — a 16x increase in parameters — only increased cost from 6 to 22 TPU v3 core-years. That is a 3.6x cost increase for 16x more parameters. Sub-linear scaling. Meanwhile, a 2.3B dense model required 235 core-years and produced lower quality than the 600B MoE. Read that again: the sparse model was 260x larger in total parameters, 10x cheaper to train, and better.

GShard used an alternating pattern: MoE layers at every other transformer block, dense layers in between. This compromise reduced communication overhead but also limited the model's ability to route tokens to specialists at every layer. It also introduced a capacity factor — a multiplier that sets a hard ceiling on how many tokens each expert can accept per batch. The formula: capacity = (tokens_per_batch / num_experts) * capacity_factor. Set the capacity factor too low and tokens get dropped — literally discarded during training, their loss ignored. Set it too high and you waste compute on padding empty slots. Typical values: 1.0 to 1.25. Another knob to tune, another source of fragility.

Dropped tokens are a jarring concept. During training, some tokens are simply... skipped. Their gradients are never computed. Their patterns are never learned from. At capacity_factor=1.0 with imperfect load balancing, GShard could drop 10-30% of tokens per batch. These tokens still exist in the training data, but the model never sees them. The capacity factor is a safety valve that prevents any single expert from being overwhelmed, at the cost of wasting training data.

GShard proved MoE could scale. But it exposed three problems that would define the next four years of MoE research. First, top-2 routing meant double the communication overhead — every token travels to two devices instead of one. Second, the capacity factor was an uncomfortable design choice — an admission that the system couldn't balance itself without hard limits. Third, the auxiliary loss for load balancing competed with the language modeling loss, creating a fundamental tension in the training objective. The scale proof was convincing. The design was not yet elegant.

Switch Transformer: radical simplification

In 2021, William Fedus, Barret Zoph, and Noam Shazeer (again — the same Shazeer who started this in 2017, who will appear in Part I for MQA, and who keeps showing up wherever efficient inference matters) published something counterintuitive. Everyone assumed you needed top-2 routing — sending each token to at least two experts for quality and stability. Two experts provide redundancy. Two perspectives catch errors. Two is a safety net.

Fedus and colleagues asked: what if you route each token to just one?

They called it the Switch Transformer. Top-1 routing. One expert per token. The benefits cascaded immediately: router computation halved, expert batch sizes halved, communication costs halved. They scaled to 1.6 trillion parameters and achieved a 7x pre-training speedup with the same computational resources.

The paper also solved a practical problem that had blocked large sparse models: numerical precision. Previous MoE implementations required float32 for stability, which doubled memory usage. Switch Transformer introduced selective precision — the router stays in full precision (because small score differences determine routing) while the experts run in bfloat16 (because their outputs are aggregated, smoothing out noise). This idea — precision matters more for routing decisions than for expert computation — directly foreshadowed DeepSeek-V3's FP8 strategy three years later.

Switch Transformer also formalized the capacity factor: capacity = (tokens_per_batch / num_experts) * capacity_factor. With a capacity factor of 1.0, each expert handles exactly its fair share. Anything above 1.0 adds buffer. Anything below means tokens get dropped. The formula made the trade-off explicit: you are choosing between wasted compute and lost data.

The simplification principle

Switch Transformer's lesson: the obvious complexity (top-2 routing, elaborate gating) wasn't necessary. One expert per token. The simpler architecture was faster, cheaper, and just as good per-FLOP. When in doubt, try the simpler routing strategy first.

Mixtral: the open-source proof

In January 2024, Mistral AI released Mixtral 8x7B — the first high-quality open-source MoE model. Eight experts per layer, top-2 routing, 47 billion total parameters, 13 billion active. It matched LLaMA 2 70B on most benchmarks with 6x faster inference. It matched or beat GPT-3.5 across every evaluated benchmark. Open weights, Apache 2.0 license.

Mixtral mattered because it proved MoE wasn't just a Google research curiosity. Anyone could download the weights, run the model, and verify the claims. The architecture used MoE at every layer (not alternating like GShard), which meant every feed-forward computation was sparse. Each of the 8 experts was a standard-sized FFN — the same size as in Mistral 7B. The "8x7B" name is slightly misleading: the model is not 56B parameters because the attention layers are shared across all experts. Total: 47B. Active per token: 13B.

The inference performance was the headline. A 47B-parameter MoE model serving with only 13B active parameters per token runs nearly as fast as a 13B dense model while achieving the quality of a 70B dense model. For users running models locally, this was transformative. You could get 70B-quality responses at 13B-speed on hardware that couldn't handle a 70B dense model at all. MoE's promise — massive knowledge with affordable compute — became tangible for the first time outside Google's datacenters.

But Mixtral used the old playbook — large experts, top-2 routing, auxiliary loss for balancing. The experts were coarse-grained, each one processing a wide variety of token types without fine specialization. It was a proof of concept for the MoE idea at production quality, not an advance in the MoE architecture itself. The innovations would come from a different lab, working under tighter constraints.

Routing Evolution — Dense vs Sparse
Mode: Dense — every token activates every expert. Maximum compute, maximum redundancy.

Watch how routing evolves. Dense lights up every column for every token — maximum compute, maximum redundancy. Top-2 (GShard) picks two experts per token, cutting active parameters to 25% of total. Top-1 (Switch Transformer) picks one expert, cutting further to 12.5%. Expert Choice inverts the direction entirely: experts reach down and grab the tokens they want, guaranteeing perfect balance at the cost of per-token determinism.

Each step in this evolution removes one assumption. Dense assumes every parameter is needed for every token — wrong, MoE showed 5.5% is enough. Top-2 routing assumes you need two expert perspectives for reliability — wrong, Switch Transformer showed one is enough. Auxiliary losses assume you must penalize the training objective for balance — wrong, DeepSeek showed a non-gradient bias suffices. Each simplification sounds risky in advance ("surely removing the second expert will degrade quality?") but each time, the simpler approach works as well or better. The pattern is consistent: MoE research has systematically overestimated the complexity needed for good routing.

DeepSeek will eventually question the last remaining assumption: that the balancing mechanism must interfere with the training objective. They will remove the auxiliary loss entirely and replace it with something that doesn't touch the gradient at all.

The sub-linear scaling math

The economics of MoE come from a mathematical property: total parameters scale linearly with the number of experts, but per-token compute stays constant (determined by top-k, not by total experts). If you double the experts from 128 to 256:

Total parameters: roughly doubles (each new expert adds its FFN weights)

Per-token compute: unchanged (still only k=8 experts fire)

Communication cost: increases slightly (router scores 256 instead of 128, tokens may travel to more distant devices)

Memory cost: increases (all 256 expert weight sets must be stored somewhere)

GShard demonstrated this concretely: 16x more parameters for 3.6x more cost. Dense scaling would have required 16x more cost for 16x more parameters. The gap between linear (dense) and sub-linear (sparse) widens as you scale up. This is why MoE becomes more attractive, not less, at extreme scale.

03 // The Routing Problem

Every MoE system faces the same existential threat, and it starts with something as innocent as a random initialization. The router, initialized randomly, happens to give Expert A a slightly higher score than Expert B for a particular token. Expert A processes that token and updates its weights. Because Expert A has now seen slightly more data than Expert B, it becomes marginally better at processing similar tokens. The router notices. It sends the next similar token to Expert A. Expert A improves again. The feedback loop tightens.

Within a few thousand training steps, the model has collapsed into a dense network wearing an MoE costume — one or two experts doing all the work, the rest dead weight. Expert A has captured 60% of all tokens. Expert B has 25%. Experts C through H share the remaining 15%, each one barely trained, barely capable, barely alive. You allocated GPU memory for 8 experts but only 2 are learning. The other 6 are dead parameters — consuming memory, contributing nothing.

The collapse is difficult to detect early and impossible to reverse once established. By the time you notice that Expert A is processing 60% of tokens (if you're monitoring that metric at all), Expert A's weights have been shaped by a fundamentally different data distribution than the other experts. You cannot simply redistribute tokens and expect the starved experts to catch up. Their weights are untrained for the tokens they would now receive. The only option is to restart the training run with different hyperparameters. At the scale of DeepSeek-V3 — 2,048 GPUs running for weeks — a failed run wastes millions of dollars.

This is expert collapse, and it has killed training runs worth hundreds of thousands of dollars. A training run that collapses mid-way through — say, at step 200,000 of a 500,000-step schedule — wastes weeks of GPU time and millions of dollars. You cannot simply restart from the collapse point because the router's weights are already corrupted. You restart from scratch, with a different balancing hyperparameter, and hope.

The death spiral

Popular experts get more tokens. More tokens make them better. Better experts get even more tokens. Without intervention, MoE converges to a system where 90% of parameters are never used. Every major MoE system — from Shazeer's 2017 paper to Mixtral — has needed some form of load balancing to survive.

The auxiliary loss trade-off

The standard fix since Shazeer's original paper: add an auxiliary loss term to the training objective. This loss penalizes imbalanced routing. If one expert gets too many tokens, the loss increases, pushing the router to spread the load. The evolution of this idea maps the evolution of the field: Shazeer (2017) used noisy gating as implicit balancing. GShard (2020) added an explicit auxiliary loss. Switch Transformer (2021) carefully tuned the loss coefficient. ST-MoE (2022) added a router z-loss for numerical stability. Each generation added another patch to the same leaky abstraction.

The problem is fundamental. The auxiliary loss fights the primary training objective. The model is simultaneously trying to learn language and balance load. These two objectives have different gradients, and those gradients pull in different directions. Too little auxiliary loss and experts collapse. Too much and the model sacrifices quality to maintain balance. Every practitioner tunes this coefficient by feel, trading off between two opposing forces.

The situation is worse than it sounds. The optimal routing for language modeling is probably not perfectly balanced. Some experts should get more tokens — because some types of knowledge are more common than others. English grammar appears in nearly every sentence. Quantum physics appears rarely. A perfectly balanced router forces the quantum physics expert to process grammar tokens it shouldn't care about. Balanced routing is not the same as optimal routing.

The Aux Loss Trade-off// Standard MoE training loss
total_loss = language_loss + alpha * aux_balance_loss

// alpha too low  -> expert collapse, dead parameters
// alpha too high -> balanced routing, degraded quality
// alpha just right -> nobody knows, you tune it

// The aux balance loss (simplified):
aux_balance_loss = N * sum(fraction_routed_i * avg_gate_prob_i)
// where N = number of experts
// Minimized when all experts get equal traffic
// But equal traffic ≠ optimal routing

Visualize the dilemma. You have two gradient signals pulling on the same router parameters. The language modeling loss says: "route this French token to the French expert, because that is where it will be processed best." The auxiliary balance loss says: "the French expert already has too many tokens this batch, route elsewhere." These gradients collide in the router's weight update. The optimizer takes a step that is a weighted average of the two directions — partially toward better language modeling, partially toward better balance. The router is being pulled in two directions simultaneously, and the compromise degrades both objectives.

The auxiliary loss coefficient alpha controls the trade-off. Typical values range from 0.01 to 0.1. At alpha=0.01, the balance signal is weak — experts may still collapse if the language loss strongly prefers certain experts. At alpha=0.1, the balance signal is strong — the router prioritizes spreading load over choosing the best expert. No value of alpha is optimal because the problem is structural: you are trying to optimize two conflicting objectives with a single set of router weights.

Expert Choice: the inversion

In 2022, Yanqi Zhou and colleagues at Google asked a question that seems obvious in retrospect but which nobody had asked in the right way: why do tokens choose experts? What if the direction were reversed?

In their Expert Choice routing, each expert picks its top-k tokens from the batch. The expert examines all available tokens, scores them, and selects the ones it is best equipped to handle. The result: guaranteed perfect balance. Every expert processes exactly the same number of tokens by construction. Important tokens might be claimed by many experts. Trivial tokens by few or none. No capacity factor needed, no tokens dropped.

Expert Choice achieved 2x training convergence speedup over Switch Transformer in their experiments. It also revealed something unexpected: some tokens are naturally processed by many experts (complex, ambiguous tokens that benefit from multiple perspectives) while others are claimed by few or none (simple tokens like punctuation that don't need expert attention). The system discovered variable computation per token — hard tokens get more compute, easy tokens get less — without anyone designing that behavior.

But Expert Choice introduced a fundamental limitation: you lose per-token deterministic routing. During autoregressive inference, where you process one token at a time, Expert Choice cannot decide which experts should handle that single token. The "experts choose" paradigm needs a full batch to work. You can train with Expert Choice but you need a different routing strategy at inference time. That mismatch between training and inference is uncomfortable.

By 2023, the routing problem had three imperfect solutions. Auxiliary losses worked but degraded quality. Expert Choice balanced perfectly but couldn't do inference. Noisy gating explored but didn't guarantee balance. The field needed something that balanced load without corrupting the training signal, and worked at both training and inference time.

The communication wall: why routing is a physical problem

Routing is not just a software decision — it is a physical one. In a distributed MoE training setup, each expert lives on a specific GPU (or group of GPUs) in the cluster. When the router decides that Token 47 should go to Expert 183, Token 47 must physically travel across the network from its current GPU to Expert 183's GPU.

This is the all-to-all communication pattern. Every token could potentially go to any expert on any device. On modern hardware, this accounts for over 40% of total training runtime. For DeepSeek-V3 specifically, the computation-to-communication ratio before DualPipe was approximately 1:1 — half the time computing, half the time moving data.

The routing problem is therefore doubly constrained: the router must assign tokens to experts that will produce good outputs (quality) while also keeping the communication pattern efficient (balance). Sending all tokens to the same expert is catastrophic for quality (other experts die) and also for communication (one GPU is overwhelmed while others idle). The routing decision affects both the model's intelligence and the cluster's hardware utilization.

04 // DeepSeek's Two Moves

By late 2023, DeepSeek — a Chinese AI lab backed by the hedge fund High-Flyer, operating under U.S. export restrictions that limited them to H800 GPUs with half the interconnect bandwidth of H100s — inherited two unsolved problems. Coarse-grained experts wasted capacity. Load balancing destroyed performance.

They solved both. And the solutions are connected by a single idea: granularity changes everything. Fine-grained experts are smaller, which means they specialize more sharply, communicate less data per dispatch, and fit more cleanly into a memory budget constrained by export restrictions. The two innovations — fine-grained segmentation and auxiliary-loss-free balancing — are published in separate papers but designed as a pair.

Move 1: 256 tiny experts instead of 8 big ones

Standard MoE (Mixtral, GShard) uses 8 to 16 large experts. Each expert is a full-sized feed-forward network — billions of parameters. When a token routes to an expert, it activates the entire FFN, even if only a fraction of that knowledge is relevant. Imagine hiring a full orchestra when you need a violinist. The violinist plays. The trumpets, drums, cellos, and flutes sit idle — but you still paid for the entire concert hall.

DeepSeek's first paper on the topic, published in January 2024 as "DeepSeekMoE: Towards Ultimate Expert Specialization in Mixture-of-Experts Language Models," proposed fine-grained expert segmentation. The idea: instead of 8 large experts, use 256 small ones. Each expert is 1/m the size of a standard FFN. Activate more of them (8 out of 256 per token) but each is narrower, cheaper to communicate, and more specialized.

The math works out cleanly. If you take a standard FFN with hidden dimension H and split it into m experts, each with hidden dimension H/m, and then activate k=8 of them, the total compute per token is (k/m) times the standard FFN cost. With m=32 (256 experts, each 1/32 the size), activating k=8 gives you 8/32 = 25% of one full FFN equivalent of compute per token. But the total parameter storage is 256/32 = 8x the standard FFN. You get 8x the knowledge capacity for 25% of the per-token compute cost. The remaining 248 experts contribute zero compute for this token. Their knowledge is stored, ready, but dormant.

The actual ratio in DeepSeek-V3 is even more extreme. With 671B total parameters and 37B active, the activation ratio is 5.5%. For every token processed, 94.5% of the model's knowledge sits idle. This sounds wasteful until you remember that the idle knowledge costs only storage (GPU memory), not compute (FLOPs). Storage is cheap. Compute is expensive. Sparsity trades cheap resources for expensive ones — exactly the trade-off you want.

The insight is that granularity enables specialization. With 8 experts, each must be a generalist — it handles too many different types of knowledge. Consider what happens when you route a French sentence to Expert 3 in an 8-expert system. Expert 3 has also learned some Python, some medical terminology, some legal language. It is a committee pretending to be a specialist. With 256 experts, each can become a true specialist. Expert 47 handles French syntax. Expert 183 handles Python list comprehensions. Expert 12 handles medical terminology. The routing decision becomes more precise because the destinations are more distinct.

Then they added one more piece: a shared expert. One expert that is always active for every token, regardless of routing. This shared expert captures common knowledge — grammar, basic reasoning, frequent patterns — that every token needs. Think of it as a generalist doctor who handles 80% of cases, with 256 specialist consultants activated on demand for the specific 20% each token requires.

The shared expert solves a subtle redundancy problem. Without it, every routed expert must independently learn basic grammar and common word patterns, wasting capacity on knowledge that should be shared. The shared expert absorbs this common substrate, freeing routed experts to specialize exclusively in their narrow domains. It is a simple architectural decision with outsized impact.

Mixtral (Coarse)

8 experts per layer. Top-2 routing. Each expert: full-sized FFN. 47B total, 13B active. Experts must be generalists.

DeepSeek-V3 (Fine)

256 routed experts + 1 shared. Top-8 routing. Each expert: 1/m sized FFN. 671B total, 37B active. Experts can specialize.

The validation: DeepSeekMoE-16B matched the performance of LLaMA 2 7B — a dense model with comparable activated parameters — at 40% of the compute. The architecture was then tested at 145B scale, confirming that the fine-grained approach held up. By the time DeepSeek-V3 deployed it at 671B with 256 routed experts across 61 transformer layers, the design was battle-tested at three scales.

What specialization looks like

Fine-grained experts don't just enable specialization in theory — you can observe it empirically. When researchers analyze which experts activate for different types of input, patterns emerge. Tokens from Python code consistently activate a different subset of experts than tokens from French prose. Mathematical notation routes to yet another set. The router learns to use the 256-expert vocabulary as a kind of implicit type system, categorizing knowledge by domain without anyone labeling the domains.

The shared expert absorbs the common substrate: articles ("the," "a"), common prepositions, basic verb conjugations, punctuation patterns. These appear in nearly every input regardless of domain. Without the shared expert, this common knowledge would be redundantly duplicated across many routed experts, wasting capacity. The shared expert functions as a deduplication layer — it extracts the universal and lets the specialists focus on the particular.

This is qualitatively different from the coarse-grained approach. In Mixtral's 8-expert system, each expert processes about 12.5% of all tokens, across all domains. Expert 3 might handle French syntax and Python indentation and medical terminology. It is a generalist committee. In DeepSeek-V3's 256-expert system, each routed expert processes about 3% of tokens on average (8 experts active per token across 256 total). The routing can be surgical: this token goes to the quantum physics expert because it contains "Hamiltonian," not because it was randomly assigned to an overworked generalist.

The combination of 256 fine-grained experts and 1 shared expert also changes the nature of the routing decision. In an 8-expert system, the router must simultaneously handle common knowledge and specialized knowledge — "should this grammar token go to Expert 1 or Expert 5?" Both are generalists, so the choice doesn't matter much. The routing decision has low information content.

In the fine-grained system, the shared expert handles all grammar, and the router's job simplifies to: "given that the shared expert already captures the basics, which 8 specialists does this specific token need?" The routing decision becomes more meaningful because the base case is already covered. The router is no longer deciding between generalists. It is selecting specialists for a specific job, with the common work already done. This is a more informative routing decision, which means the router can learn more useful patterns from the same training signal.

There is also a resilience benefit. If the router makes a poor routing decision in an 8-expert system — sending a token to the wrong generalist — the output quality degrades significantly because that expert handles a large fraction of the token's total computation. If the router makes a poor routing decision in the fine-grained system, one of the 8 selected specialists might be suboptimal, but the shared expert still provides a correct baseline, and the other 7 specialists are likely still relevant. The architecture is more robust to routing errors because no single routing decision controls a large fraction of the output.

Move 2: Auxiliary-loss-free load balancing

Here is the move that changed the economics. DeepSeek looked at the auxiliary loss that every other MoE system used and asked: what if we just... didn't? What if we replaced the loss with something that doesn't interfere with training at all?

The mechanism is deceptively simple:

Each expert i gets a bias term bi, initialized to zero. The bias is a scalar, not a learned parameter — it is not part of the model's weights and has no gradient. When the router scores each expert for a given token, the bias is added to the affinity score to determine which experts are selected (top-k). But — and this is the critical detail, the hinge on which the entire mechanism turns — the actual gating weight (how much that expert's output contributes to the final result) uses the original, unbiased score.

The Bias Trick// For each token t and expert i:
affinity_i  = dot(token_embedding, expert_i_centroid)
selection_i = affinity_i + b_i   // bias nudges SELECTION
weight_i    = softmax(affinity_i) // weight uses PURE affinity

// After each step, update biases:
if expert_i overloaded:  b_i -= gamma
if expert_i underloaded: b_i += gamma

The bias changes which experts get picked. It does not corrupt how much each expert's output counts. The router might pick Expert 47 because (affinity + bias) is highest. But Expert 47's contribution to the final output is weighted by affinity alone — the bias term disappears from the gating weight computation. The model's actual output is identical to what it would produce if it had "naturally" chosen Expert 47 without the bias. Quality preserved, balance achieved.

Contrast this with the auxiliary loss approach. Auxiliary loss adds a competing gradient to the training objective. Two gradients pulling in different directions — learn language, balance load. The optimizer tries to satisfy both and satisfies neither fully. DeepSeek's bias has no gradient at all. It is adjusted by a simple rule after each step, completely outside backpropagation. The training loss remains pure. The optimizer sees only one objective: learn language.

The subtlety is worth emphasizing because it is easy to miss. The bias affects the selection decision: which experts get picked. It does not affect the value signal: how much each expert's output counts. The gating weight — the coefficient that determines Expert 47's influence on the final output — is computed from the raw affinity score, without the bias. So the bias can redirect traffic (preventing collapse) without corrupting the quality of the expert's contribution (preserving training signal). Selection and weighting are decoupled.

Auxiliary Loss Approach

Two competing gradients in the optimizer. Language loss pulls one direction, balance loss pulls another. The router's weights are a compromise that fully satisfies neither objective.

Bias Approach

One gradient (language loss only). The bias is a separate, non-gradient adjustment that nudges routing decisions. The optimizer sees a clean, single-objective landscape.

Why it works

The bias is a nudge, not a force. It affects selection (which experts are chosen) but not weighting (how much they contribute). This preserves the purity of the expertise signal while preventing the death spiral of collapse. Zero interference with the training objective. The first solution to expert collapse that costs nothing in model quality.

The difference is a single additive term in the routing decision. No auxiliary loss. No competing gradients. No capacity factors. No dropped tokens. Just a bias that nudges the selection toward balance while leaving the quality signal untouched.

Why the separation of selection and weighting matters

Consider a concrete example. Token "entanglement" has the following affinity scores:

Expert 12 (physics): 0.85
Expert 47 (chemistry): 0.72
Expert 183 (math): 0.68
Expert 7 (general): 0.65

Without bias, Expert 12 is selected first (highest score). But Expert 12 is overloaded, with bias = -0.25. After adding bias:

Expert 12: 0.85 + (-0.25) = 0.60 (demoted)
Expert 47: 0.72 + 0.10 = 0.82 (promoted)

Expert 47 gets selected instead. But when computing the output, the weight for Expert 47 uses the original 0.72 score, not 0.82. The model says: "I'm sending you to Expert 47 because it has capacity, but I'm weighting Expert 47's output by how relevant it actually is, ignoring the routing nudge."

This separation is why DeepSeek's approach doesn't degrade quality. The gating weights — which determine how much each expert contributes to the output — are always based on genuine affinity. The bias only changes which experts get to participate, not how much they matter.

05 // The Full Stack

DeepSeek-V3 did not invent most of the ideas in this article. MoE came from Jacobs in 1991. Latent attention compression existed in DeepSeek-V2. FP8 training had been studied for years. Multi-token prediction was a known technique. What DeepSeek did was combine them into a system where each innovation compensates for the weaknesses of the others. The genius is in the composition, not in any single component.

This is a systems engineering achievement, not a singular research breakthrough. Each technique was published, understood, and available to anyone. MLA was in DeepSeek-V2. FP8 training had been studied at smaller scales. Multi-token prediction was a known technique from Meta and others. DualPipe builds on standard pipeline parallelism ideas. The insight was not any one of these components. The insight was that they fit together — that MLA's memory savings create room for MoE's 256 experts, that FP8's reduced precision makes DualPipe's double-parameter requirement affordable, that MTP's speculative decoding turns MoE's natural structure into an inference accelerator.

The analogy is an engine, not a breakthrough. An engine does not depend on any single revolutionary part. It depends on every part being precisely designed to work with every other part. The camshaft timing matches the piston stroke. The fuel injection matches the compression ratio. Change one part without adjusting the others and the engine fails. DeepSeek-V3 is an engine. Remove any one piece and the economics collapse.

MLA: freeing the memory

Multi-head Latent Attention, inherited from DeepSeek-V2 and covered in depth in Part I of this series, compresses keys and values into a low-rank latent space — 512 dimensions down from 7,168. The KV cache shrinks by roughly 93%. This seems like an attention optimization. It is. But in DeepSeek-V3, it serves a second purpose that matters even more.

Why does MLA matter for MoE? Because MoE models have 256 expert weight sets per layer, across 61 layers, that must physically reside in GPU memory. That is an enormous memory footprint. MLA frees the GPU memory that those expert weights need by compressing the attention cache. Without MLA, you simply cannot fit 256 fine-grained experts on H800s alongside a functional KV cache. The two innovations are co-dependent: MLA makes MoE physically possible at this scale.

MLA also improves MoE serving economics. MoE models are designed to serve many users simultaneously — that is the whole point of high-throughput inference. Each user's context requires a KV cache. With standard multi-head attention on a 671B model, that cache would be prohibitive. MLA makes high-throughput multi-user serving economically viable.

FP8: halving the arithmetic

DeepSeek-V3 is the first model to validate FP8 training at extreme scale — 671 billion parameters. The numbers behind FP8: E4M3 format gives you 4 exponent bits and 3 mantissa bits. That is a dynamic range of about 240 and a precision of roughly 1 part in 8. For context, BF16 gives about 1 part in 128. You are sacrificing 16x precision. The question is: does it matter?

The answer depends on where you apply it. DeepSeek's insight, echoing Switch Transformer's selective precision from three years earlier: the router must stay in BF16 because routing decisions are numerically sensitive. A score difference of 0.001 can change which expert is selected, which changes the entire computation. The experts themselves tolerate FP8 because their outputs are combined by gating weights, smoothing out quantization noise through aggregation.

The selective precision map is detailed and deliberate:

ComponentPrecisionWhy
Embedding layerBF16Token representations must be accurate
Output headBF16Final logits determine token selection
Router / gatingBF16Small score differences change routing
NormalizationFP32Running statistics need high precision
Attention operatorsBF16Softmax is precision-sensitive
Expert FFNsFP8Outputs aggregated, noise averages out
Activation dispatchFP8Halves cross-node communication bytes
Optimizer statesBF16Momentum and variance need precision

This is not "FP8 training" in the naive sense. It is a precision budget allocated according to each component's sensitivity to quantization noise. The most precision-sensitive components (router, normalization, output head) keep full precision. The most volume-heavy components (expert FFNs, activation dispatch) use FP8. The boundary is drawn exactly where the cost-quality trade-off changes slope.

Validation: relative loss error versus BF16 baseline stayed below 0.25% throughout 14.8 trillion tokens of training. Activations are cached and dispatched in FP8, which has a critical secondary effect: it reduces all-to-all communication volume for MoE. When tokens travel between devices to reach their assigned experts, they travel as FP8 tensors instead of BF16 tensors. Half the bytes. Half the communication time. One precision decision, two payoffs.

MTP: double-duty prediction

Standard language model training predicts one token ahead: given tokens 1 through N, predict token N+1. DeepSeek-V3 adds a separate 14B-parameter module that simultaneously predicts token N+2. This is Multi-Token Prediction, and it serves two distinct purposes.

During training, predicting further ahead forces the model to build deeper internal representations. If you only predict one token ahead, you can sometimes succeed with shallow pattern matching — "the" is likely after "in" regardless of broader context. Predicting two tokens ahead requires understanding the actual structure of the sentence. The MTP loss weight starts at 0.3 for the first 10 trillion tokens, then decreases to 0.1 as the model matures. The richer training signal is most valuable early, when representations are still forming.

During inference, the MTP module enables speculative decoding. The model drafts two tokens at once, then verifies them in a single forward pass. If the speculative tokens are correct (which they often are, since the model was trained to predict them), you get nearly double throughput for free. The MTP module is essentially a built-in draft model — no need for a separate smaller model to generate candidates. One module, two purposes, zero wasted parameters.

The MTP architecture is designed to be removable. The main model (671B) can operate independently at inference time without the MTP module (14B). This means the MTP module is purely additive during training — it improves the main model's representations through the multi-token prediction signal — and optional during inference. If you want maximum throughput, you enable speculative decoding. If you want minimum memory, you drop the MTP module. The design gives you the option without forcing the cost.

DualPipe: hiding the communication wall

With 256 experts spread across nodes, every MoE layer requires all-to-all communication: dispatch tokens to the right experts, gather results back. For DeepSeek-V3, this creates a roughly 1:1 computation-to-communication ratio — half the training time is spent moving data, not computing. On H800s with their restricted interconnect bandwidth, this ratio is even worse.

DualPipe is a bidirectional pipeline parallelism algorithm that exploits a key observation: attention computation and MoE communication use different hardware resources. Attention uses the tensor cores. All-to-all dispatch uses the network interface. They can happen simultaneously if you schedule them correctly.

DualPipe divides each processing step into four components: attention, all-to-all dispatch, MLP (expert computation), all-to-all combine. While one set of micro-batches does forward computation (attention + MLP), another simultaneously handles backward communication (all-to-all dispatch and combine). Micro-batches feed from both ends of the pipeline — bidirectional scheduling.

The resource allocation is precise. Only 20 streaming multiprocessors (out of ~132 on an H800) are dedicated to communication. Those 20 SMs are enough to saturate both InfiniBand and NVLink bandwidth. The remaining 112 SMs compute. The result: near-zero effective communication overhead. The pipeline bubble formula — (PP/2 - 1)(F&B + B - 3W) — is significantly smaller than standard pipeline parallelism. Peak activation memory is approximately (PP+1)/PP times baseline, which is near 1x overhead for reasonable pipeline depths.

DualPipe requires two copies of model parameters for bidirectional scheduling. This sounds expensive — two copies of 671B parameters? — until you realize two things. First, FP8 halved the per-copy memory, so two FP8 copies take the same space as one BF16 copy. Second, the alternative (no DualPipe) means 40-50% of training time is spent waiting for communication, which would more than double the training cost from $5.6M to roughly $11M. Storing an extra copy for $0 (net) versus wasting $5M on idle compute is not a hard trade-off. The innovations compensate for each other, again.

The constraint that created the innovation

DeepSeek had H800s, not H100s. Half the interconnect bandwidth. Every optimization in this stack — DualPipe, FP8 dispatch, MLA memory savings — was born from that constraint. The $5.576M training cost is not a miracle of genius. It is what happens when hardware limitations force every component to justify its existence.

The compensation relationships are what make this a system rather than a collection of parts. MoE creates a communication bottleneck (all-to-all dispatch across nodes). DualPipe hides that communication behind computation. But DualPipe needs two copies of model parameters, which doubles memory. FP8 halves parameter memory, making two copies affordable. FP8 risks precision loss, but MLA's compressed representations are more tolerant of quantization noise. MTP adds 14B parameters and extra training compute, but MoE's sparse activation absorbs the cost — the extra parameters are small relative to 671B, and the sparse compute budget has headroom.

Remove MLA, and the expert weights don't fit in memory. Remove FP8, and DualPipe's double-parameter requirement breaks the memory budget. Remove DualPipe, and communication overhead eats 40-50% of training time, roughly doubling the cost to $11M+. Remove MTP, and you lose the speculative decoding that makes inference competitive with dense models. The system holds together or falls apart as a unit.

This is why DeepSeek-V3 is hard to replicate by cherry-picking individual innovations. You cannot take "just the MoE" and bolt it onto a different model. The MoE works at this scale because MLA made room for 256 expert weight sets. The MoE is affordable because FP8 halved the arithmetic and DualPipe hid the communication. The system was designed as a system. Each component assumes the others exist.

Training stability at scale

DeepSeek reports that the entire 14.8-trillion-token pre-training run completed without a single irrecoverable loss spike or rollback. This is remarkable for a 671B MoE model in FP8. For context, the original GPT-4 training reportedly required multiple restarts due to loss spikes. LLaMA 3.1's training involved checkpoint restarts. Large-scale training instability is the norm, not the exception.

Several design choices contribute to DeepSeek-V3's stability. The auxiliary-loss-free balancing prevents the router from oscillating between conflicting gradients (a known source of training instability in MoE). The selective precision keeps the router in BF16, avoiding the numerical instability that FP8 would introduce in routing decisions. The shared expert provides a stable baseline of computation that every token receives, regardless of routing noise — even if the router makes a poor decision for a particular token, the shared expert provides a floor of quality that prevents catastrophic individual predictions.

Stability at scale is not a glamorous property, but it is an economic one. A training run that requires restarts wastes the GPU hours between the last checkpoint and the failure point. For a 2,048-GPU cluster running at $2/GPU-hour, every hour of wasted training costs $4,096. A restart from a checkpoint 10 hours back costs $41,000 in wasted compute alone, not counting the lost training progress. A major loss spike that requires restarting from a checkpoint 24 hours back costs $98,000.

Zero restarts over a 56-day training run is worth millions in saved waste. The DeepSeek team's meticulous approach to precision boundaries, load balancing, and gradient purity paid dividends not just in model quality but in training reliability. The cheapest GPU hour is the one you never waste. In a world where a single training run costs millions and takes months, reliability is itself a form of efficiency.

Anyone with 2,048 H800s and $5.6M could train this model from the published recipe. That is a fundamentally different economic regime from the $100M+ training runs at frontier labs.

06 // Sparsity as Worldview

Mixture of Experts is not just an architecture trick. It is a principle about how intelligence should be organized: not everything needs to activate for every input. A model can store 18x more knowledge than any single token ever uses. The efficiency comes from conditional computation — not from doing less computation overall, but from doing the right computation for each input. The model knows more. It just doesn't think about all of it at once.

This is a fundamentally different philosophy from the dense scaling era, and the difference has economic consequences. Dense scaling says: throw more parameters at the problem and activate all of them. Every token pays the full cost, regardless of whether the question is about quantum mechanics or the word "the." Doubling the model's knowledge doubles the per-token compute. The cost of intelligence scales linearly with knowledge.

Sparse scaling says: throw more parameters at the problem and activate only what matters. The word "the" gets cheap, fast routing through common-knowledge experts. A question about quantum entanglement activates the physics specialists. Doubling the model's knowledge (adding 256 more experts) does not double the per-token compute (still only k=8 fire). The cost of intelligence grows sub-linearly with knowledge. Same ambition, radically different economics.

DeepSeek-V3 stores 671 billion parameters of knowledge — medical terminology next to Python syntax next to French grammar next to quantum mechanics — and for each token, a router selects the 37 billion parameters that are relevant. The rest sleep. The activation ratio is 5.5%. Ninety-four and a half percent of the model's knowledge is dormant for any given token. That is not waste. That is efficiency.

The numbers tell the story of a field learning to be less wasteful. The 26-year gap from Jacobs 1991 to Shazeer 2017 was about infrastructure. The explosion since then — GShard (2020), Switch Transformer (2021), Expert Choice (2022), DeepSeekMoE (2024), DeepSeek-V3 (2024) — was about refinement. Top-2 routing to top-1. Sixteen experts to 256. Auxiliary loss to no loss at all. Each paper removed one source of waste, one unnecessary computation, one redundant parameter activation.

There is a broader lesson here about the relationship between constraint and innovation. DeepSeek operated under U.S. export restrictions that limited them to H800 GPUs — hardware with half the interconnect bandwidth of the H100s available to American labs. Every innovation in this article — fine-grained experts that reduce per-expert communication volume, bias-based balancing that doesn't waste gradient compute, FP8 dispatch that halves communication bytes, DualPipe that hides whatever communication remains — was shaped by that constraint.

The $5.576M training cost is not a miracle. It is what happens when you cannot afford to waste anything. When your interconnect bandwidth is halved, you must find a way to halve your communication volume (FP8 dispatch) and hide the remaining latency (DualPipe). When your GPU memory is constrained, you must compress your KV cache (MLA) and reduce your weight precision (FP8). When you cannot simply buy more hardware, you must extract more value from every FLOP you already have (MoE, MTP).

Necessity was the author of this architecture. The export restriction intended to slow Chinese AI development forced DeepSeek to optimize relentlessly. Every wasted byte, every idle cycle, every unnecessary computation became a target. The techniques they developed are not specific to H800s. They would make H100 training cheaper too. American labs with unrestricted H100 access could apply the same stack and achieve even lower costs, because the optimizations compound with higher base bandwidth. The irony may be lasting: the constraint that was supposed to create an advantage for American AI may have produced a playbook that makes all AI training cheaper, everywhere.

The scaling lesson

The MoE timeline encodes a pattern that repeats across this entire series. A good idea appears decades before its time (Jacobs 1991, like Hopfield networks in the 1980s, like attention in the early 2000s). The idea waits for hardware. Hardware arrives. A flurry of refinement begins. Each refinement removes one source of waste. The end state is always simpler than the intermediate steps suggested it would be.

GShard needed 2,048 experts and top-2 routing. Switch Transformer showed top-1 was enough. Expert Choice showed you could invert the routing direction entirely. DeepSeekMoE showed 256 tiny experts beat 8 large ones. DeepSeek-V3 showed you could drop the auxiliary loss entirely. Each step removed complexity. The most advanced MoE system is also the simplest in some ways: pure language modeling loss, a single bias term for balancing, and a shared expert for common knowledge. No capacity factors. No z-losses. No noisy gating.

The H800 constraint and what it taught

The H800 is a modified H100 designed for export to China under U.S. Department of Commerce restrictions. The key difference: NVLink bandwidth is capped at 400 GB/s (vs 900 GB/s on the H100), and the chips cannot use the same high-speed NVSwitch interconnect fabrics available domestically. For MoE training, where all-to-all communication is the primary bottleneck, this is a devastating limitation — roughly half the interconnect bandwidth.

Every innovation in DeepSeek-V3's training stack responds to this constraint:

FP8 dispatch: reduces communication bytes by 50% (compensating for 50% less bandwidth)

DualPipe: hides whatever communication time remains behind computation

Fine-grained experts: smaller expert activations mean smaller tensors traveling between devices

Custom all-to-all kernels: hand-tuned to minimize SM usage for communication (20 SMs vs potentially 40+ with generic implementations)

The irony: the export restriction intended to slow Chinese AI development may have forced DeepSeek to develop more efficient training techniques than would have been necessary with unrestricted H100 access. Constraint bred innovation. The techniques DeepSeek developed are not H800-specific — they would make H100 training cheaper too.

The papers, in order

Jacobs, Jordan, Nowlan, Hinton (1991) — "Adaptive Mixtures of Local Experts." The seed. Divide, gate, specialize. Demonstrated on vowel discrimination. Dormant for 26 years.

Shazeer et al. (2017) — "Outrageously Large Neural Networks." Revived MoE with sparsely-gated layers on LSTMs. 137B parameters, >1000x capacity. The resurrection.

Lepikhin, Lee et al. (2020) — GShard. 600B parameters on 2,048 TPUs. Sub-linear cost scaling. The scale proof.

Fedus, Zoph, Shazeer (2021) — Switch Transformers. Top-1 routing. 1.6T parameters. 7x speedup. The simplification.

Zhou et al. (2022) — Expert Choice routing. Experts choose tokens, not vice versa. 2x convergence. The inversion.

Mistral AI (2024) — Mixtral 8x7B. First open-source high-quality MoE. 47B total, 13B active. Matched LLaMA 2 70B at 6x speed.

DeepSeek (2024) — DeepSeekMoE. Fine-grained experts (256 small vs 8 large). Shared expert trick. Matched LLaMA 2 7B at 40% compute.

DeepSeek (2024) — DeepSeek-V3. Auxiliary-loss-free balancing. Full-stack integration. 671B parameters, $5.576M training cost. The synthesis.

The open question

MoE makes the FFN sparse. Only 8 of 256 expert sub-networks fire per token. But the attention layer remains dense. Every token still attends to every other token in its context window — O(n²) comparisons for a sequence of length n. For a 128K context window, that is 16.4 billion comparisons per token per layer. The attention layer does not know about sparsity. It does not route. It does not specialize. It computes the full pairwise attention matrix every time.

The sparsity revolution has so far stopped at the FFN boundary. The question is whether it can cross into attention. What if you could apply the same principle — not every computation is necessary for every input — to the attention mechanism itself? What if most of those 16.4 billion comparisons are redundant, and a smarter routing policy could skip them?

The precedent is encouraging. At every stage of MoE's development, the field overestimated the amount of computation needed. Dense transformers activate every parameter. MoE showed you could activate 5.5%. Top-2 routing was considered necessary for quality. Top-1 turned out to be enough. Auxiliary losses seemed unavoidable for stability. A simple bias term replaced them with zero quality cost. Capacity factors seemed necessary to prevent overflow. Fine-grained experts made them unnecessary. Each time, less computation and less mechanism sufficed.

If that pattern holds — and three decades of evidence suggests it does — the full attention matrix may be the next density assumption to fall. Every token comparing against every other token, O(n²) comparisons, quadratic cost. The sparsity principle says: most of those comparisons are probably redundant. A smarter routing policy could skip them. Part VI explores that frontier.
